## 0. Status vocabulary and standing warnings

Every claim below carries one of:

| tag | meaning |
|---|---|
| `[P]` **paper-asserted** | the source's own authors report it in the paper |
| `[I]` **independently reproduced** | someone *other than the original authors* re-ran it and reported the result; the reproducer is named with a URL |
| `[U]` **upstream report** | GitHub issue/PR, model card, or project blog. Not peer-reviewed. Real, retrievable, but weaker |
| `[!]` **UNRETRIEVED** | I could not retrieve it. **Not usable downstream** |

Three standing warnings that apply to almost everything in the literature on this topic:

**W1 — The literature is overwhelmingly decoder-only and autoregressive.** YaRN, NTK, ABF,
Position Interpolation, LongRoPE, SelfExtend, RULER, NoPE, *Lost in the Middle*: all evaluated on
causal LMs on generation/perplexity/retrieval. Sliding-window semantics, "extrapolation", and even
the meaning of "using" a long context are different in a bidirectional encoder with a
non-autoregressive decision head. **No source I retrieved measures RoPE scaling on a bidirectional
encoder for a classification decision.** Treat every transfer as a hypothesis.

**W2 — Nominal context length is not effective context length.** This is not a side note; it is the
central replicated finding of the field, and our 1024-token collapse is an instance of it rather
than an anomaly. RULER `[P]`: *"While all models claim context size of 32K tokens or greater, our
results indicate that only half of them can effectively handle sequence length of 32K by exceeding
a qualitative threshold. Moreover, almost all models fall below the threshold before reaching the
claimed context lengths."* — 17 long-context LLMs, 13 synthetic tasks, 8×A100, greedy decoding
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654). Men et al. `[P]` name the same thing *"superficial long context
capability, in which case the model can only preserve low perplexity but loses the ability to
retrieve long context information"* (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14591).

**W3 — Perplexity is not a valid metric for this question.** Men et al. `[P]` fine-tuned
Llama2-7B-Base on 32k context across RoPE bases and found *"base=2e5 achieves the best perplexity,
but the accuracy of Long-eval is very low, which indicates the limitations of perplexity in
evaluating long context capabilities"*, and *"although the difference in perplexity between
different bases is negligible, the accuracy of Long-eval varies significantly"*
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14591). **Implication for our harness:** any intervention in this space
must be scored on the decision task with real labels. A masked-LM loss or PPL movement is not
evidence, in either direction. This is the single most important methodological constraint I found.

**W4 — Truncation and attention-reachability are different failure mechanisms, and the prior art says
the difference decides whether this entire literature applies to us.** Longformer gives both the law
and the remedy. The law, §3.1: *"In a transformer with ℓ layers, the receptive field size at the top
layer is ℓ × w (assuming w is fixed for all layers)."* The remedy for classification-shaped tasks is
to make the decision token **global and symmetric**: *"Importantly, we make this attention operation
symmetric: that is, a token with a global attention attends to all tokens across the sequence, and all
tokens in the sequence attend to it. … For example for classification, global attention is used for
the [CLS] token."* (https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150, §3.1) `[P]`

The distinction this forces:

- If a decision fails because the evidence is **outside the processed input** (truncation), no
  attention-pattern intervention can help, and every result in §2 and §3 is off-target. The
  intervention that matters is input coverage.
- If it fails because the evidence is **inside the input but not reachable** by the decision token,
  then §3 is directly on point and Longformer's symmetric-global-token design is the published
  remedy.

**Which one we have is a fact about our harness, not about the literature, and I did not measure
it.** Our brief describes the failure as *"with a 1024-token budget … for documents ≥ 2000 tokens"*,
which reads as truncation. If that reading is right, the 1024↔8192 gap is an input-coverage effect,
and prior art predicts mask-level interventions will do little — which is precisely what the measured
widening results in §2.5 found. **Confirm which mechanism is at play before funding any mask-level
arm.** Flagged to `engineer` and `scribe`; it is a question about our harness, and I am not asserting
an answer.

## 1. RoPE scaling — does any of it help a model **already trained at the target length**?

**Short answer from prior art: no, and the implementations are built so that it cannot.** This is a
*definitional* answer, not an empirical one, which is why it is unusually strong.

### 1.1 The decisive result, and it is provable from the code, not measured `[I]`

HF `transformers` (the version our `encoder/config.json` declares is 5.0.0) implements RoPE scaling in
`modeling_rope_utils.py`. The **`dynamic`** (NTK) branch contains this:

```python
# src/transformers/modeling_rope_utils.py :: _compute_dynamic_ntk_parameters
# seq_len: default to max_position_embeddings, e.g. at init time
if seq_len is None:
    seq_len = config.max_position_embeddings
elif isinstance(seq_len, torch.Tensor):
    seq_len = torch.maximum(
        seq_len,
        torch.tensor(config.max_position_embeddings, dtype=seq_len.dtype, device=seq_len.device),
    )
else:
    seq_len = max(seq_len, config.max_position_embeddings)

base = base * ((factor * seq_len / config.max_position_embeddings) - (factor - 1)) ** (dim / (dim - 2))
```

https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/modeling_rope_utils.py

`seq_len` is **clamped up to `max_position_embeddings` before it is used**. With
`max_position_embeddings = 8192`, every input of length ≤ 8192 is clamped to exactly 8192, so the
inner expression evaluates to `factor · 1 − (factor − 1) = 1`, and therefore

```
base_new = base · 1^(dim/(dim−2)) = base
```

**Independent of `factor`.** This is an algebraic identity, not a measurement: with
`rope_type: "dynamic"`, RoPE scaling is a *provable no-op* on every sequence this model can accept.
The docstring says the same thing in words — *"If seq_len <= max_position_embeddings, this factor
has no effect"* — though its next sentence repeats the `<=` condition where it evidently means `>`;
the code is unambiguous even where the prose is not, and I am citing the code.

The other branches do **not** have this protection, and this is the whole taxonomy:

| `rope_type` | applies below `max_position_embeddings`? | mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| `default` | no scaling at all | — |
| `dynamic` | **provably no** — `seq_len` clamped to 8192 | shown above |
| `linear` | **yes, unconditionally** | `inv_freq = 1/(base^(2i/d)); inv_freq /= factor` — `seq_len` is never read |
| `yarn` | **yes, unconditionally** | frequency ramp from `original_max_position_embeddings` + an attention temperature `mscale(factor)` applied to cos/sin |
| `longrope` | **yes, unconditionally** | per-dimension factors from a supplied list |

So of the five options in our implementation, **one is a no-op inside the window and the rest rescale
positions the model was trained on.** YaRN additionally changes the attention softmax temperature at
*all* lengths (`attention_factor = get_mscale(factor)`, defaulting to `0.1·ln(factor)+1`), which is a
concrete mechanism for the short-context damage LongRoPE2 measured (§1.3) — the temperature moves
even when the sequence does not exceed the trained length.

**This settles required-coverage item 1 for our implementation without an experiment**, and it is the
strongest single result in this report. It does *not* settle whether a *fine-tuned* scaling (i.e. a
new training run) could help at ≤8192 — see §1.6 for why that is also the wrong place to look.

### 1.2 Dynamic scaling is defined to be the identity inside the trained window `[P]`

The canonical fix for short-context damage is to make the scale factor a function of the *current*
sequence length rather than a constant, reverting to no scaling at all below the trained length:

> `s = l'/L` if `l'/L > 1`, **`s = 1` otherwise**

EleutherAI, *Extending the RoPE* (https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/), the authors' own exposition of
YaRN. The same page states the failure mode of the alternative: *"A common problem is that the
model may experience a **flat reduction of performance at lengths less than L** or an abrupt
degradation on longer sequences starting at L′+1 tokens."*

**Read that against our situation.** Our encoder was trained at `max_position_embeddings = 8192`,
which is also our ceiling. Every input we will ever score is at length ≤ L. Under the dynamic rule
that the field converged on, the correct scale factor for all of our inputs is **s = 1** — i.e. do
nothing. A *fixed* scaling factor applied inside the window is precisely the configuration the
EleutherAI authors identify as causing a flat reduction in performance.

### 1.3 LongRoPE2's entire contribution is undoing the short-context damage the other methods cause `[P]`

This is the strongest and most direct negative result I found, because it is a *quantified*
comparison of YaRN / NTK / LongRoPE against the un-extended model, on the original short window:

- *"extending Phi3-mini to 128k results in **MMLU score drops of 7.56, 4.34, and 3.52 points for
  YaRN, NTK, and LongRoPE, respectively**"* (Fig. 2c).
- *"YaRN and NTK show performance drop of **−15.2% and −9.3%** on Phi3-mini, with declines of
  **−21.15 and −14.55 absolute points on GSM8K**"* (Table 4).
- *"LongRoPE2 is the only RoPE rescaling method that can retain over 97% of the original
  short-context performance."*

https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20082 (Microsoft; LongRoPE2, 2025).

The fix they had to invent is itself the tell: LongRoPE2 uses **mixed context window training** and
then, *at inference*, *"original RoPE is used if the input is within the short context; otherwise,
rescaled RoPE is applied"* `[P]`. An ablation confirms the mechanism: *"removing mixed context
window training results in a significant drop in performance on regular short-context tasks, as
expected"* `[P]`.

So even the state of the art in 2025 does not apply rescaled RoPE to inputs that fit in the
original window. **It switches it off.**

### 1.4 The original LongRoPE had to add a recovery pass for exactly this `[P]`

LongRoPE (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13753) lists as its third contribution: *"(iii) we **readjust
LongRoPE on 8k length to recover the short context window performance**"*. It also gives the
mechanism: *"when extending to extremely long context windows, the attention becomes dispersed as
it's spread thinly across numerous token positions, **degrading performance on the original short
context**"*, and reports that *"existing methods such as PI, and non-uniform NTK and YaRN cause
perplexity to spike after 2× extension"* `[P]`.

### 1.5 Training-free position remapping also costs accuracy inside the window `[P]`

SelfExtend (https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01325) remaps out-of-range positions by a floor/group
operation, needing no fine-tuning. Even so: *"with grouped attention, the PPL is **a little higher**
than the original LLMs, which is expected"*, and *"Once the length of a sequence is longer than the
new 'context window' … the PPL explodes again."* The method is a genuine no-fine-tuning win
*beyond* the trained length and a small loss *within* it — consistent with §1.2. The same paper
notes the general hazard of the alternative: *"finetuning risks degrading existing strong
performance on shorter sequences from pretraining or overfitting models to the tuning set"* `[P]`.

### 1.6 Where RoPE scaling does help — and the bound on it `[P]`

To be fair to the positive results: RoPE base/scale changes unambiguously help *beyond* the trained
length. Chen et al., *Scaling Laws of RoPE-based Extrapolation*, ICLR 2024
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05209) report reaching 1M-token context from a 16K training length
using base 500, and >100K from a 4K training length using base 1e6. Two caveats that matter to us:

1. **Larger bases have a hard extrapolation ceiling and then degrade fast**: *"the extrapolation
   with larger bases has a clear limit where language modeling perplexity stays consistent. Beyond
   this limit, the extrapolation performance declines significantly … compared to Dynamic NTK,
   RoPE tuned with larger bases **degrades much quicker beyond its extrapolation upper bound**"*
   `[P]`.
2. **The tuning phase is what matters, not the inference constant**: *"base 10000 yields the worst
   extrapolation performance when fine-tuned"*, and *"fine-tuning a RoPE-based LLM with either a
   smaller or larger base in pre-training context length could significantly enhance its
   extrapolation performance"* `[P]`. Every gain in this paper is a **fine-tuning** result.

### 1.7 The base only bounds context from *below*; there is no known upper bound `[P]`

Men et al. (NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14591) derive a *lower* bound on RoPE base for
a target context length, validated on Llama2-7B and Baichuan2-7B fine-tunes and on a 2B model
trained from scratch. Their Table 2:

| context length | 1k | 2k | 4k | **8k** | 16k | 32k | 64k | 128k |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| lower bound on base | 4.3e3 | 1.6e4 | 2.7e4 | **8.4e4** | 3.1e5 | 6.4e5 | 2.1e6 | 7.8e6 |

Their own caveat, verbatim: *"this boundary is not very strict because the stacking of layers in
LLMs allows the model to extract information beyond the single layers' range."* And the paper is
explicit that the other direction is unknown: *"the existence of the upper bound for RoPE's base
remains an open question."* `[P]`

**This is context, not a claim about us.** It is a theory-derived bound validated on decoder-only
LLMs at 4k→32k, on a *different* architecture family. What it does establish is the *direction*:
more base is not free, and past a point there is no published account of what it buys.

### 1.8 The RoPE-scaling mechanism is implementable per-layer-type — but only in the harmful direction

Read from the HF implementation that our config declares (`transformers_version: 5.0.0`):

```python
# src/transformers/models/modernbert/modeling_modernbert.py
self.rope_type = {}
for layer_type in self.layer_types:
    rope_params = self.config.rope_parameters[layer_type]
    ...
    if self.rope_type[layer_type] != "default":
        rope_init_fn = ROPE_INIT_FUNCTIONS[self.rope_type[layer_type]]
    curr_inv_freq, curr_attention_scaling = rope_init_fn(self.config, device, layer_type=layer_type)
```

https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/models/modernbert/modeling_modernbert.py
(`ModernBertRotaryEmbedding.__init__`). `[I]` — I read the source; this is a code fact, not a claim
about outcomes.

Two consequences:
- RoPE scaling **can** be applied separately to `full_attention` and `sliding_attention` layers,
  and `yarn` / `linear` / `dynamic` are all reachable. Mechanically this is a one-line config edit.
- `self.original_max_seq_len = config.max_position_embeddings` = 8192. So a scaling factor > 1
  rescales positions that are, by construction, all inside the trained range. That is exactly the
  configuration §1.1 and §1.3 identify as damaging. `"dynamic"` is the only `rope_type` that
  becomes identity below 8192 by construction.

### 1.9 `[!]`-adjacent: the only public YaRN extension of a ModernBERT-class encoder is not usable evidence

`llm-semantic-router/modernbert-base-32k` is, as far as I can retrieve, the only public instance of
YaRN applied to a ModernBERT-family **bidirectional encoder**: `rope_scaling_type: yarn`,
`rope_scaling_factor: 4.0`, fine-tuned 1B tokens at 32K on one MI300X (~4 h), base
`answerdotai/ModernBERT-base` (https://huggingface.co/llm-semantic-router/modernbert-base-32k). `[U]`

**I do not consider its evaluation usable, and downstream must not cite its numbers.** Reported:
*"Perplexity @ 32K: 1.00"* for **both** the fine-tuned model and the unmodified base, and 1.00 again
at 8192/16384/24576/32768; *"Basic MLM Accuracy 100%"* for both; and a distance-based retrieval
table that is non-monotonic (80% @64 tokens, 27% @512, 20% @8192). A perplexity of exactly 1.00 at
every length for both a trained and an untrained model is not a plausible language-modelling
measurement, and the card states no dataset, split, seed, or metric definition. The card's headline
claim — extension "while preserving the base model's capabilities" — is **not supported by the
numbers on the card**.

What it *does* establish, and all I would take from it: (a) extending an 8192-trained bidirectional
encoder to 32K is considered worth a YaRN fine-tune in practice; (b) the recipe used realistic
fine-tuning compute (1B tokens, a datacentre GPU) — i.e. **not** an inference-time-free lunch. This
is consistent with §1.2–§1.5 and with ModernBERT's own extension cost (§1.10).

### 1.10 What mmBERT itself did — read from the primary sources, not inferred

mmBERT (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06888; model card https://huggingface.co/jhu-clsp/mmBERT-base),
the encoder lineage behind our checkpoint:

- *"We also change the RoPE values to handle up to 8192 tokens (i.e. **theta of 160k**) for **global
  and local layers**."* `[P]` — note this is a **divergence from English ModernBERT**, whose local
  layers use theta 10,000 and only global layers use 160,000 (ModernBERT Table 4,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663). This matches the `rope_parameters` block in our
  `encoder/config.json`, where both `full_attention` and `sliding_attention` carry
  `rope_theta: 160000`.
- Context extension was a **600B-token mid-training stage** (1024 → 8192), not an inference-time
  trick. Table 8 of the paper lists `Max Sequence Length: 1024->8192`, `Rotary Embedding Base:
  10k -> 160k`, `Sliding Window: 128`, `Global Attention Every N Layers: 3`. `[P]`
- **mmBERT's paper contains no sliding-window ablation, no global/local-pattern ablation, and no
  long-document classification evaluation at 8192.** I searched the full text for `sliding`,
  `window`, `local att`, `global att`, `ablation`; the only hits are the static Table 8 entries. `[I]`

**Implication for us:** the question "is mmBERT's ±64 local window the bottleneck at 8192" is
**not answered anywhere in the published record for this model family**. That is a gap, and it means
our measurement would be novel — it also means we cannot shortcut it by citation.

### 1.11 Verdict on required-coverage item 1

**The headline result is §1.1 and it is not empirical:** in the implementation we actually run,
`dynamic` RoPE scaling is an algebraic identity for every sequence length this model accepts, and
`linear` / `yarn` / `longrope` rescale in-range positions unconditionally. The table below is the
empirical corroboration from the literature.

| intervention | does prior art support it for a model already trained at L_target? | strongest source |
|---|---|---|
| `rope_type: "dynamic"` | **Provably does nothing at ≤8192** — `seq_len` is clamped to `max_position_embeddings` before use. Not a judgement call. | https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/modeling_rope_utils.py |
| `linear` / `yarn` / `longrope` | **Actively rescale in-range positions**, unconditionally. YaRN also moves the attention softmax temperature at *all* lengths. | same file; https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ |
| YaRN | **No.** Identity below L by design; damages short context when applied fixed; the fix is to switch it off below L. | https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ , https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20082 |
| NTK-aware | **No**, and worst of the group on short context (−4.34 MMLU, −9.3% overall, −14.55 pts GSM8K on Phi3-mini). Also *worse than plain PI after fine-tuning*. | https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20082 , https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ |
| ABF (base adjust) | **No published basis at ≤L.** The base bound is a *lower* bound; the upper bound is explicitly open. | https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14591 |
| Position interpolation | **No inside the window.** PI's own documented cost is *"perplexity slightly increases for short sequences compared with the original pretrained model"* after long-context fine-tuning. | https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ |
| Self-extend | **No inside the window.** Raises PPL below L; helps only beyond it. | https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01325 |
| *any* scaling **beyond** L (=8192) | Plausible, bounded, and **expensive** — the ModernBERT-family example needed a 1B-token fine-tune on datacentre hardware; mmBERT needed 600B tokens. | https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06888 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663 |

## 2. Sliding-window widening at inference on a model trained with a smaller window

Full treatment in `R-002.md`. The findings from my own search that most constrain us:

### 2.1 What `local_attention = 128` means in our exact implementation `[I]`

```python
# src/transformers/models/modernbert/modeling_modernbert.py
if config.layer_types[layer_idx] == "sliding_attention":
    # config.sliding_window = local_attention // 2 (half-window size, e.g. 64 for local_attention=128)
    # +1 is needed because flash attention sets inclusive boundaries
    self.sliding_window = config.sliding_window + 1
else:
    self.sliding_window = None

self.is_causal = False
```

https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/models/modernbert/modeling_modernbert.py

Three facts, all code-level: half-window = `local_attention // 2` = **64**; the FlashAttention
window is `64 + 1` on inclusive-boundary grounds; and `is_causal = False` for **every** layer, i.e.
the sliding attention is **bidirectional**. This independently confirms the ±64 bidirectional
reading in our brief from a retrievable source.

### 2.2 The one published window-size study on long-document classification — headroom is tiny `[P]`

*Revisiting Transformer-based Models for Long Document Classification* (EMNLP Findings 2022,
https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683) varies Longformer's local attention window and **retrains each
variant** on MIMIC-III (micro-F1 on dev; speed in processed samples/s):

| local window | micro-F1 | train speed | test speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32 | 67.9 ± 0.3 | 9.9 (2.9×) | 45.6 (2.8×) |
| **64** | **68.1 ± 0.1** | 8.8 (2.6×) | 41.4 (2.5×) |
| 128 | 68.3 ± 0.3 | 7.4 (2.1×) | 34.1 (2.1×) |
| 256 | 68.4 ± 0.3 | 5.5 (1.6×) | 25.4 (1.6×) |
| 512 | 68.5 ± 0.3 | 3.5 (1.0×) | 16.3 (1.0×) |

Their own summary: *"Small local attention windows are effective and efficient … even using a small
window size, the micro F1 score on MIMIC-III development set is still close to using a larger
window size. We observe the same pattern on ECtHR and 20 News."* `[P]`

**Crucial scope limit — do not over-read this.** This is a *training-time* ablation: each row is a
separately trained model. It is therefore **not** evidence about inference-time widening, which is
our actual question. What it bounds is the **headroom**: in the one published measurement of this
axis on long-document classification, an 8× wider window bought +0.4 F1 while costing 2.5× test
throughput. If that transfers *at all*, inference-time widening has almost nothing to win and a
train/test mismatch to lose. The transfer is not established.

### 2.3 ModernBERT's own pattern ablation was flat — but not at 8192 `[P]`

Appendix D of the ModernBERT paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663):

> *"We explored the use of alternating global/local attention, with global attention every 3 layers
> and local attention over a 128 token sliding window otherwise. This setup **yielded identical
> downstream performance when compared to the use of global attention in every layer**, even at 100
> billion tokens, while resulting in major speedups."*

**Caveat I must attach:** the paper does not state the sequence length for this ablation (Appendix D
says most ablations ran at the 8–20B-token scale). The main pretraining phase at that point was
1024 tokens. **This is therefore not evidence about 8192-token behaviour**, and it should not be
cited as such. It is evidence that at *short* lengths the window width is not the binding
constraint — which is the same regime the MIMIC-III ablation covers.

Where ModernBERT *does* hint at a long-context window cost, it is explicitly speculative `[P]`:
on MLDR long-context retrieval without long-context fine-tuning, ModernBERT *"performs noticeably
worse than GTE-en-MLM"* and the authors write *"We plan to explore multiple potential explanations
for this phenomenon in future work, including **the impact of local attention**, or GTE-en-MLM
having spent a larger part of its pretraining compute budget on longer sequence lengths."* That is
an open question from the authors of the architecture, not a finding.

### 2.4 Implementation trap: some production paths ignore the window entirely `[U]`

`vllm-project/semantic-router` issue #3406 (opened 2026-09-03, closed 2026-09-16),
*"[Bug] ModernBERT flash-attention path ignores the sliding window and the padding mask"*:
*"`ModernBertAttention::forward` receives `pad_mask` and `window` and passes neither to the flash
path … A layer is local when `layer_id % global_attn_every_n_layers != 0`, so with the usual value
of 3, two layers out of every three are local. The dense and chunked paths restrict those layers to
±`local_attention / 2`; **the flash path lets every query attend to every key** … Both are silent:
`flash_attn` succeeds and returns a wrong-but-plausible tensor."* Resolved by PR #3769.
https://github.com/vllm-project/semantic-router/issues/3406 `[U]`

**This is an engineering hazard, not a result, and it is not about our stack** — it is a Rust/candle
implementation. But it establishes that in this model family, "which attention pattern actually ran"
is kernel-dependent and can fail **silently**. Any window-widening arm must first verify that the
baseline path actually applies the window it claims to. → handed to `engineer`.

### 2.5 CORRECTION — inference-time window change **has** been measured, and widening does not help

**This subsection replaces an earlier claim of mine that the inference-time question was unmeasured.
That claim was wrong**, and it was falsified by `R-002.md` (sub-agent A). I re-retrieved each source
myself before writing this; all three are verified against the full text, not a search snippet. The
correction is recorded rather than quietly overwritten.

**(a) Gemma 2 — the one paper that changes a trained model's sliding window at inference `[P]`**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00118, Table 10). Verbatim: *"we show that we can change the sliding
window size of the local attention layers of the models during inference with moderate impact on
perplexity. Adjusting the size of the sliding window can thus be a leverage for slight inference
speed gain."* 9B validation perplexity: **4096 → 1.63, 2048 → 1.63, 1024 → 1.64.**

Read the direction carefully: Gemma 2's local layers are trained at **4096**, and the table only goes
**down**. So this is evidence that *narrowing* a trained window is well tolerated — in a
local/global hybrid, i.e. our architecture class — and it is **not** evidence about widening. Any
citation of Gemma 2 Table 10 as support for widening past the trained window is a misreading.

**(b) StreamingLLM — widening with weights fixed was measured and is not benign `[P]`**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453, Table 6; 400K-token concatenated PG19 test set, same weights
within each row). Perplexity as the cache window grows:

| model | small | | | large |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falcon-7B | 13.61 (4+252) | 12.84 (4+508) | 12.34 (4+1020) | **12.84** (4+2044) |
| MPT-7B | 14.12 (4+252) | 14.25 (4+508) | 14.33 (4+1020) | **14.99** (4+2044) |
| Pythia-12B | 13.17 | 12.52 | 12.08 | 12.09 |
| Llama-2-7B | 9.73 (4+508) | 9.32 (4+1020) | 9.08 (4+2044) | **9.59** (4+4092) |

Authors: *"Contrary to intuition, increasing the cache size doesn't consistently lower the language
modeling perplexity. This inconsistency shows a potential limitation where these models might not
maximize the utility of the entire context they receive."* `[P]`

**Scope limit, stated plainly:** these are *dense-attention-trained* models run with a windowed
cache — the **opposite** mismatch direction to ours (we would widen a narrow-window-trained model).
So this bounds expectations and kills the "widening is free" intuition; it is not a direct analogue.

**(c) SWAA — the closest controlled no-retraining study, and it says the window is not the lever `[P]`**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10411, "Sliding Window Attention Adaptation for Efficient Long-Context
LLMs Without Pretraining"; Qwen3-4B on LongMemEval_24k). Verbatim from Table 1 and the analysis:

- Full attention baseline: **73.0** (thinking) / 62.0 (non-thinking).
- 2k window, all layers SWA, no other change: **3.2 / 11.0** — a collapse of the same character as
  the one we are investigating.
- **Widening 2k → 4k alone barely moves it (38.2 → 38.0)** while FA decode is in use.
- Recovery comes from **restoring global attention paths**: interleaved FA layers `[1,3,5,…]` at a 2k
  window gives 13.4 / 18.4; the full combination (FA decode + keep-first-k + interleaving + 4k
  window) reaches **73.0**, matching the full-attention baseline *without retraining*.

The authors' own conclusion is the finding: *"Sliding window size affects, but is not the decisive
role"*, while interleaving and FA decode are *"the dominant drivers."* `[P]`

**What this changes for us, and what it does not.** The corrected picture is sharper than the one I
first wrote: the inference-time question is *not* unmeasured, and where it is measured, **widening
the window is close to a no-op or mildly harmful, while restoring global attention is what
actually recovers performance.** That is a materially different design signal from the one in my
first draft, and it is the strongest single pointer in this report toward reallocation rather than
widening. `R-002.md` carries the full treatment; my §3.2 is corrected on the same basis.

**Still `UNRETRIEVED`:** any study that widens the window on a model *trained with a narrow window*
(i.e. the mismatch direction that actually matches us). All three sources above change the window on
a model trained with full or much wider attention. That specific experiment still appears unmeasured.

## 3. Global/local layer reallocation, post-hoc

Full treatment in `R-002.md`. What I found:

### 3.1 Two independent negative results for "more global attention" `[P]`

**(a) ModernBERT** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663), quoted in §2.3: global-every-layer vs
global-every-3-layers gave *"identical downstream performance"*. The model *shipped* with 2/3 of its
layers local. Same caveat: ablation sequence length unstated, scale 8–20B tokens.

**(b) Longformer / MIMIC-III** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683): *"Figure 6 shows that **adding
more tokens using global attention does not improve F1 score**, while a small number of additional
global attention tokens can make the training more stable."* They also test *how* to pick the extra
global tokens — position-based (evenly spaced) vs content-based (TF-IDF top-K) — and find
*"the position based approach is more effective than content based"* `[P]`.

### 3.2 CORRECTION — post-hoc reallocation **is** established; it works at layer and head granularity

**This subsection replaces an earlier claim of mine that post-hoc layer swapping had no prior art.
That claim was wrong**, falsified by `R-002.md` (sub-agent A). I re-retrieved both sources myself.
The correction is recorded rather than quietly overwritten — and the corrected content points the
opposite way from my first draft, which matters for the design.

**(a) SWAA: interleaving full-attention layers post-hoc, no retraining `[P]`**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10411 — the same paper as §2.5c). Starting from a model adapted to
all-SWA with a 2k window, **interleaving FA layers** (`[1,3,5,…]`) plus FA decode plus keep-first-k
recovers **73.0 vs a 73.0 full-attention baseline** on LongMemEval_24k, *without any pretraining*.
Isolated effects from Table 1: interleaving alone at a 2k window gives 13.4 / 18.4 against a
naive-SWA 3.2 / 11.0, and *"Interleaving Layers + FA Decode is markedly stronger (row 13), recovering
most of the gap for the thinking model."* `[P]` The authors are explicit that interleaving and FA
decode are *"the dominant drivers"* and that *"Sliding window size affects, but is not the decisive
role"* `[P]`.

**This is the strongest positive post-hoc-reallocation evidence in the literature, and it is a
direct pointer for us:** in their setting the intervention that worked was *restoring global
attention*, not touching the window.

**(b) DuoAttention: post-hoc reallocation at HEAD granularity `[P]`**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10819). Attention heads split into **retrieval heads**, which
*"are critical for processing long contexts and require full attention across all tokens"*, and
**streaming heads**, which *"primarily focus on recent tokens and attention sinks"* and tolerate a
constant-length cache. The mechanism is *"a lightweight, optimization-based algorithm with synthetic
data to identify retrieval heads accurately"*, and the cost is explicit: *"This training phase is
efficient, requiring only the gate values to be trainable—**leaving all other model parameters
frozen**—thus allowing it to be completed within several hours on an **8 GPU node**."* `[P]`

Three things to take from it, including the negatives:
- Reallocation is **not training-free** — it needs an optimization pass over per-head gates (frozen
  weights, but still an 8-GPU-node run). That is outside our stated budget.
- It is **head-granular, not layer-granular.** Nobody found layer granularity sufficient; the
  working unit was the head.
- The **asymmetry is load-bearing**: *"modifying retrieval heads severely damages performance, while
  constraining streaming heads has minimal impacts"* `[P]`. Getting the classification wrong is
  catastrophic in one direction and free in the other — so a reallocation arm needs to identify
  which layers are load-bearing before it perturbs them, not after.

**(c) Still `UNRETRIEVED`:** a study that swaps `layer_types` (local↔global) on a **bidirectional
encoder** checkpoint. Everything above is decoder-only causal attention, where "global" also implies
causality. The mechanism is now established to *exist* and to *work in decoder LMs*; its transfer to
a bidirectional encoder with a decision head remains untested.

**Net effect on the design question.** My first draft read "untested, so treat as risky"; the
corrected reading is "tested in adjacent settings, and the version that worked was *adding global
attention back*, which is the same axis as our §3.1 negatives but at a different granularity
(layer-interleaving and per-head gates rather than 'make more layers global')." The two §3.1
negatives were training-time ablations of *how many* layers are global; SWAA and DuoAttention are
post-hoc restorations of global *paths*. Those are not the same intervention and should not be
collapsed. Full treatment in `R-002.md`.

### 3.3 One architecture fact that changes the risk profile — from the published configs `[I]`

English ModernBERT uses **different RoPE bases for local and global layers** (10,000 vs 160,000,
Table 4 of https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663). Swapping a layer's attention pattern in *that* model
would hand it a positional-encoding regime its weights were never trained under, in addition to a
different mask.

**mmBERT does not have that property.** Its paper states theta 160k *"for global and local layers"*
`[P]` (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06888), and our `encoder/config.json` has
`rope_parameters.full_attention.rope_theta == rope_parameters.sliding_attention.rope_theta ==
160000`.

So in mmBERT, converting a sliding layer to full attention changes **the mask only** — the RoPE
base is already the one that layer was trained with. That removes one specific confound that would
have applied to English ModernBERT. It does **not** make the intervention safe, and it says nothing
about whether the resulting model is any good: the layer's *weights* were still trained under a
±64 receptive field. Flagged for `math` and the experiment design; **not** a prediction of outcome.

### 3.4 The window is *learned*, not just applied — Longformer's own account `[P]`

This is the most direct prior-art argument against any inference-time attention-pattern change, and
it comes from the paper that introduced the window. Longformer §5:

> *"Ideally, we would like to train our model on the largest window size and sequence length we can
> fit in a modern GPU memory. However, **we found that the model needs a large number of gradient
> updates to learn the local context first, before learning to utilize longer context.** To
> accommodate this, we adopt a staged training procedure where we increase the attention window size
> and sequence length across multiple training phases … We train the model over 5 total phases with
> starting sequence length of 2,048 and ending sequence length of 23,040."*

https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150. The window is not a free inference-time hyperparameter in this
line of work; the authors found the *schedule* of the window affects whether the model learns to use
long context at all.

**And their one attempt to change an attention pattern on a pretrained checkpoint without retraining
degraded performance**, which they attributed to exactly the mismatch in question. Footnote 6 of the
same paper, on adding dilated attention to a few heads of a RoBERTa-initialised model:

> *"Adding dilation on a few heads as in §4.1 **hurt performance, likely because it is not compatible
> with the pretrained RoBERTa weights.** Retraining such model from scratch might be needed to improve
> performance."*

Note the scope precisely: the *main* pattern change (full → sliding window) was made to work, but
**only alongside continued MLM pretraining** from the RoBERTa checkpoint. The un-retrained variant
hurt. So the accurate reading is: **pattern changes in this family were only ever shown to work with
further training behind them**; where a pattern change was applied to frozen pretrained weights, it
hurt.

### 3.5 A genuine conflict in the record — I am not going to resolve it by picking a side

| source | claim about global attention |
|---|---|
| Longformer, https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150 | *"Through ablations and controlled trials we show **both attention types are essential** – the local attention is primarily used to build contextual representations, while the global attention allows Longformer to build full sequence representations for prediction."* `[P]` |
| Longformer / MIMIC-III, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683 | *"adding more tokens using global attention **does not improve F1 score**"* `[P]` |
| ModernBERT, https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663 | global-every-layer vs global-every-3-layers gave *"identical downstream performance"* `[P]` |
| BigBird, https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14062 | *"BigBird-etc, with **expanded global tokens consistently outperforms all other models**."* `[P]` |

These are not all measuring the same thing, and the differences are informative rather than
contradictory-in-fact:

- Longformer's "both are essential" is about **having** global attention vs not; the later MIMIC-III
  result is about **adding more** of it. Different question, and both can be true.
- BigBird's "expanded global tokens" are **task-structured** tokens — a global token for the CLS,
  one for the question, and one per sentence, up to 320–430 of them
  (https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14062, appendix). That is an *inductive bias about the task* being
  injected via the mask, not a general increase in attention capacity. Citing BigBird as evidence
  that "more global attention helps" would be a misreading.
- ModernBERT's and the MIMIC-III results are both **training-time** ablations, and ModernBERT's is at
  an unstated sequence length (see §2.3).

**What I take from the conflict:** nobody has cleanly isolated "attention-pattern capacity" from
"task-structure inductive bias" for an encoder at 8192, and the closest clean measurements
(MIMIC-III, ModernBERT) are flat. The honest conclusion is that the prior is **weak-to-neutral**, not
that the literature supports the intervention.

### 3.6 Longformer's own remedy for a decision token: make it global **and symmetric** `[P]`

This is the most design-relevant mechanism in the report, and it is stated explicitly in the paper
that introduced the alternating pattern (https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150 §3.1):

> *"In a transformer with ℓ layers, the receptive field size at the top layer is ℓ × w (assuming w is
> fixed for all layers)."*
>
> *"Importantly, we make this attention operation **symmetric**: that is, a token with a global
> attention attends to all tokens across the sequence, and **all tokens in the sequence attend to
> it**. … For example for classification, **global attention is used for the [CLS] token** … Since the
> number of such tokens is small relative to and independent of n the complexity of the combined local
> and global attention is still O(n)."*

`[P]`. Note what symmetry buys: a global decision token is reachable **from** every document token in
one hop, so the ℓ × w receptive-field bound does not govern the path into it. Longformer chose this
design *specifically* for the classification case, which is structurally the case we are in — a small
set of marker tokens that must aggregate evidence from the whole document.

Two consequences, and a boundary:

- **If our markers participate in the full-attention layers, the published design already supplies
  the symmetric path**, and the interesting question becomes whether 8 of 22 layers is enough rather
  than whether the pattern is right in kind. `math` should work this out; the governing arithmetic is
  the **longest run of consecutive sliding layers**, not total depth, because one global layer
  resets reachability. Stated as arithmetic on the published config, `[DERIVED]` not measured — I am
  not asserting an outcome.
- **This is also the mechanism-level reason the §2.5 widening results are unsurprising.** If
  reachability is supplied by the global layers, widening a local window from ±64 to ±64·k changes
  nothing about whether a distant token can reach the marker through a global layer. `R-003.md`
  derives the per-direction local reach for an all-local stack; the derivation is labelled `[DERIVED]`
  there and belongs to `math`, not to me.
- **Boundary:** Longformer's evidence for the symmetric-global remedy is **decoder-free but
  classification-only on its own benchmarks**, and no source I retrieved measures the marker-token
  reachability question on mmBERT or any ModernBERT-family encoder (§7).

## 4. Cross-attention / hierarchical hybrids, and long-doc classification evidence

Full inventory in `R-003.md`. The finding from my own pass that most constrains our design is a
**direct conflict in the published record**, and the conflict is informative.

### 4.1 Against: simple 512-token truncation beat the long-context models `[P]`, `[I]`

*Efficient Classification of Long Documents Using Transformers* (Amazon; ACL 2022 short;
https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11258) is a head-to-head evaluation of four families — truncation,
efficient self-attention (Longformer), chunk representations (ToBERT), and key-sentence selection
(BERT+TextRank, BERT+Random, CogLTX) — re-running existing methods, often with their reported
hyperparameters. This is a **third-party evaluation**, i.e. `[I]`-flavoured evidence about
Longformer/ToBERT/CogLTX, though not a reproduction by me.

Table 2 (accuracy % for Hyperpartisan/20NewsGroups; micro-F1 % elsewhere):

| model | Hyperpartisan | 20NewsGroups | EURLEX | **Inverted EURLEX** | Book Summary | Paired Book Summary |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BERT (truncate @512) | **92.00** | 84.79 | **73.09** | 70.53 | 58.18 | 52.24 |
| BERT+TextRank | 91.15 | **84.99** | 72.87 | **71.30** | 58.94 | 55.99 |
| BERT+Random | 89.23 | 84.65 | 73.22 | **71.47** | **59.36** | 56.58 |
| Longformer (@4096) | **95.69** | 83.39 | 54.53 | 56.47 | 56.53 | **57.76** |
| ToBERT (hierarchical) | 89.54 | **85.52** | 67.57 | 67.31 | 58.16 | 57.08 |
| CogLTX | 94.77 | 84.63 | 70.13 | 70.80 | 58.27 | 55.91 |

Authors' conclusion `[P]`: *"more sophisticated models (Longformer, ToBERT, CogLTX) do not
outperform the baseline models across the board"* and *"the simplest BERT baseline that truncates
documents up to the first 512 tokens shows competitive performance overall, outperforming the
majority of models for Hyperpartisan, 20NewsGroups and EURLEX. It is only the Paired Book Summary
dataset where the BERT baseline performed particularly worse than other models."* Also: *"ToBERT's
reported performance was the highest for 20NewsGroups, but we were unable to reproduce the results
due to its memory constraints."*

**Caveat that materially weakens the table above — added after `R-003.md` cross-checked it, and it
should stop anyone headlining these gaps.** The same paper's appendix states that only **one run per
learning rate** was performed and that *"lr 0.005 was used for Longformer only"*. That is a serious
confound: Longformer's poor EURLEX/Inverted-EURLEX numbers may reflect an untuned or mistuned
learning rate rather than the architecture. `R-003.md` records the gap as **unsettled**; I agree, and
**the −18.6 μ-F1 Longformer gap should not be cited as a settled result.** The qualitative finding —
that sophisticated long-doc models do not dominate truncation — survives across six datasets and is
also what §5.1's LexGLUE and BigBird evidence shows, but the *magnitude* on EURLEX does not.

**The construction that matters most to us** `[P]`: *"A document in the EURLEX dataset contains a
legal text divided into several sections, and the first two sections (header, recitals) carry the
most relevant information for classification. **We invert the order of the sections so that this key
information is located toward the end of each document** (Inverted EURLEX). This creates a dataset
particularly challenging for models that focus only on the first 512 tokens."*

That is the closest published analogue to our late-evidence failure that I found. On it, the
long-context sparse-attention encoder (Longformer, 56.47) **lost badly** to plain truncation (70.53)
and to chunk-and-select baselines (71.30 / 71.47). `[P]`

### 4.2 For: on genuinely long clinical/legal documents, more context did help `[P]`

The same year, *Revisiting Transformer-based Models for Long Document Classification* (EMNLP
Findings 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683) concludes the opposite: *"Our results show that
processing more tokens can bring drastic improvements comparing to processing up-to 512 tokens"*,
with MIMIC-III and ECtHR as the primary evidence.

**The two papers critique each other directly, and the critique is about document length** `[P]`:
2204.06683 writes of the earlier line of work, *"they experiment and report results on non-ideal
long document classification datasets, i.e., documents on the IMDB dataset are not really long –
fewer than 15% of examples are longer than 512 tokens; while the Hyperpartisan dataset only has
very few (645 in total) documents."* And 2203.11258's own dataset statistics are modest: mean BERT
token counts 368.8 (20NewsGroups) to 1,148.6 (Paired Book Summary), with only 14.7%–75.5% of
documents exceeding 512 tokens (§Table 1 of that paper).

**Implication for our protocol (the actionable part).** Whether long-context handling beats
truncation is **length-distribution-dependent**, and both papers are consistent with: *on documents
whose mass is near the window, truncation and chunk-and-select win; on documents whose mass is far
beyond the window, long-context handling wins.* Our own observed regime — collapse at ≥2000 tokens
with a 1024 budget, recovery at 8192 — sits in the second regime. **But this also means any
benchmark we pick with a median length near our budget will not be able to distinguish our arms**,
and a benchmark with a short-ish tail will make the truncation control look good for reasons that
have nothing to do with our mechanism. Benchmark selection is therefore a load-bearing decision,
not a formality. → `protocol.md` / `scribe`.

### 4.3 Hierarchical details worth having `[P]`

From https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683, all on MIMIC-III / ECtHR / 20 News / Hyperpartisan:
- *"there is no optimal segment length across both MIMIC-III and ECtHR. Small segment length works
  well on MIMIC-III, and using segment length greater than 128 starts to decrease the performance.
  In contrast, the ECtHR dataset benefits from a model with larger segment lengths."*
- Context fragmentation is real and cheaply mitigated: overlapping segments (first ¼n tokens of each
  segment taken from the previous one) *"can easily improve the effectiveness of the model."*
- Splitting by document structure (paragraph-level, following Chalkidis et al. 2022) *"does not
  improve performance on the ECtHR dataset. On MIMIC-III, splitting based on document structure
  substantially underperforms evenly splitting the document."*
- Prior negative results they cite: *"Ji et al. (2021a) and Gao et al. (2021) reported negative
  results with a hierarchical Transformer with a segment length of 512 tokens on the MIMIC-III
  dataset."*

### 4.4 Latent bottlenecks (Perceiver / Perceiver IO): the option with **no** evidence on our task `[P]`, `[I]`

This is a required-coverage item, so here is the honest answer: **neither Perceiver paper has ever
been evaluated on long-document classification, and their language evaluations top out far below our
budget.**

- Perceiver (https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03206) is evaluated on **images, point clouds, audio, video,
  and video+audio** — `[I]` full-text search finds no long-document text classification. Its
  headline language claim is *not* about long text.
- Perceiver IO (https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14795) does evaluate language, but at *"input sequence
  lengths of **512 SentencePiece tokens or 2048 UTF-8 bytes**"* `[P]`. That is roughly 512 tokens —
  **16× below our 8192 ceiling**. `[I]` A full-text search for `long document`,
  `document classification`, `hyperpartisan` in that paper returns **zero** hits.
- The GLUE comparison is at 512 tokens: *"Perceiver IO applied on SentencePiece tokenized input
  sequences slightly outperforms a strong BERT baseline applied on the same inputs (81.2 vs 81.1)"*,
  and on bytes it reaches 81.8 `[P]`.

**What the architecture actually costs — the important correction.** "Latent bottleneck" is often
read as "constant cost in input length". It is not. Perceiver's own accounting `[P]`:

> *"This results in an architecture with complexity 𝒪(MN + L·N²)"* — where M is input length, N is
> the latent count, L the depth. *"the resulting cross-attention operation has complexity 𝒪(MN)."*

So cross-attention remains **linear in input length**, with the latent count N as the constant.
Measured against a full-attention encoder at 8192 this is still a large saving — with N = 512
latents, 8192·512 ≈ 4.2M attention entries per cross-attention layer versus 8192² ≈ 67M for full
self-attention, ~16× less per layer — but it is not free, and the paper notes the tradeoff
explicitly: *"more cross-attends leads to better performance, but increases the computational
requirements of the model because it increases the number of layers with linear dependence on the
input size"* `[P]`. Perceiver IO's stronger claim applies to the *latent* stack only: *"latent
attention is independent of both input and output sizes."*

**Implication, stated as a gap not a recommendation.** The latent-bottleneck family is attractive on
cost for a fixed budget, but for **long-document classification** it is unmeasured at any length
near ours, and every published Perceiver/Perceiver-IO result trains the architecture end-to-end.
`UNRETRIEVED`: any published result that bolts a Perceiver-style latent bottleneck onto a **frozen**
pretrained encoder for long-document classification. Anyone proposing this arm for us is proposing
something without prior art in either direction, and should budget it accordingly. Full inventory of
the cross-attention / hierarchical line, including LED and Funnel/Hourglass, is in `R-003.md`.

### 4.5 Funnel / Hourglass: hierarchy buys an **equal-compute trade**, not long-context capability — and compression alone *loses* `[P]`

Both are named in the task, so both need a precise statement, because the usual citation of them is
wrong in a way that matters to us.

**Funnel-Transformer** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03236) is *"an encoder that gradually reduces the
sequence length of the hidden states as the layer gets deeper"*, so that savings can be
*"re-invest[ed] … in constructing a deeper or wider model"* `[P]`. Its headline result is
*"with comparable or fewer FLOPs, Funnel-Transformer outperforms the standard Transformer"* `[P]` —
i.e. **the comparison is at matched FLOPs, and the mechanism is trading sequence resolution for
depth.** It is not a claim that hierarchy helps you handle longer inputs at fixed capacity.

The paper's own ablation makes this explicit and is a negative result for the mechanism on its own:

> *"When we only compress the sequence length without increasing the depth (and #Params), F-TFM
> **could suffer from some performance loss** in certain settings on the GLUE datasets."* `[P]`

**So a "compress mmBERT's sequence and keep everything else" intervention is contradicted by
Funnel's own ablation**, while a "compress and spend the savings on depth" intervention is a
different, much larger piece of work. Funnel's evaluations are GLUE, 7 sentence-level sentiment/topic
classification sets (IMDB, AD, DBpedia, Yelp-2, Yelp-5, Amazon-2, Amazon-5), RACE and SQuAD — no
long-document classification `[I]`.

**Hourglass / "Hierarchical Transformers Are More Efficient Language Models"**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13711) is an **autoregressive decoder LM** with U-Net-style pool/upsample
shortening, evaluated on enwik8 character LM and ImageNet32 generation. Its claim is again
equal-compute: *"Hourglass improves upon the Transformer baseline **given the same amount of
computation** and can yield the same results as Transformers more efficiently"* `[P]`. It contributes
one framing worth keeping — the reason sparsity alone is not enough: *"sparse attention mechanisms
reduce the complexity of self-attention but **still force the model to operate on the sequence of the
same length as the input**"* `[P]`. But it is a decoder, and it is not evidence about long-document
classification.

**Neither Funnel nor Hourglass was trained as a post-hoc addition to a frozen pretrained encoder**;
both are architectures trained (largely) from scratch. Neither is evaluated at 8192 for
classification. `UNRETRIEVED`: a Funnel- or Hourglass-style head attached to a frozen pretrained
encoder for long-document classification.

### 4.6 LED (chunked encoder + cross-attention decoder): real, but the wrong task and not a bottleneck `[P]`

LED is the encoder-decoder member of this family, and it is **defined inside the Longformer paper**
(https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150 §7) — it has no standalone arXiv entry (see the correction in §5).

Its configuration is the part worth knowing, because it is often cited loosely:

> *"The encoder uses **local attention with window size 1,024 tokens** and global attention on the
> first `<s>` token. **The decoder uses full attention to the entire encoder** and previously decoded
> locations."* `[P]`

Three things follow, none of which support the usual reading:

1. **Its local window is 1,024 tokens — 16× wider than mmBERT's ±64.** So LED is not evidence that a
   ±64 window suffices; it is evidence that this family chose a much wider one.
2. **The decoder is not a latent bottleneck.** It cross-attends to the *entire* encoded sequence, so
   the cost that Perceiver removes is still present — LED gets its linearity from the *encoder's*
   sparse pattern, not from a compressed interface. Its per-token cost is therefore unlike the
   Perceiver family's.
3. **It is a seq2seq model evaluated on summarization**, on arXiv (90th-percentile document length
   14.5K tokens), scored with ROUGE. LED-large at 16K, *"merely initialized from BART, with no
   additional pre-training"*, reaches SOTA on arXiv, *"slightly outperforming BigBird"* `[P]`.
   **I retrieved no LED-style cross-attention head evaluated on long-document classification.**

There is also a cheap-extension datapoint in the same section worth registering as a contrast: LED
extends BART's **learned absolute** position embeddings from 1K to 16K by *"repeatedly copying BART's
1K position embeddings 16 times"* `[P]`. That is a crude replication trick that only makes sense for
a learned position matrix; it has no RoPE analogue, which is a reminder that "context extension"
recipes do not transfer across positional-encoding families.

**Reproducibility of the two derived sets.** Both are *constructions over public data*, so they can

- **Inverted EURLEX** — reorder EURLEX sections so the header/recitals (which carry the decisive

**Practical note for `protocol.md`.** Section 4.2 shows the truncation-vs-long-context verdict is
length-distribution-dependent. Of the public sets above, only EURLEX (51.3% > 512) and Paired Book
Summary (75.5%) have enough mass beyond our budget to separate arms; 20 Newsgroups (14.7%) and
LEDGAR would make the truncation control look strong for reasons unrelated to our mechanism.
MIMIC-III — the dataset in the study that found a *large* long-context benefit — is the one we
cannot have. This is a real limitation on how far we can validate against the literature and should
be stated in the protocol rather than worked around.

**There is no reference number to beat, and this is worth stating plainly.** The ModernBERT paper
evaluates long context **only as retrieval** (English MLDR) and as inference efficiency. Its
classification evaluation is GLUE plus a handful of short-context tasks, and a full-text search of
the paper for `Hyperpartisan`, `PubMed`, `BigPatent`, `MAVEN`, `LEDGAR`, `EURLEX`, `20News`, `IMDB`
and `BookSum` returns **zero** hits `[I]`. mmBERT's paper likewise reports XTREME/GLUE-class
classification and long-context *retrieval*, not long-document classification. So:

- the brief's suggested "long-sequence document classification from the ModernBERT paper" does not
  exist as a benchmark in that paper, and I could not retrieve one elsewhere;
- **we cannot benchmark against the model family's own long-document classification number, because
  there isn't one.** Any target we set is ours to justify, which raises rather than lowers the
  burden on `protocol.md`.

### 5.1 Current reported numbers, and the one that most constrains us `[P]`

**The most useful single number I retrieved:** on **ECtHR Task A**, using flat truncated BERT instead
of the hierarchical variant costs **−12.2 points** (Task B: −10.6; SCOTUS: −3.5). This is reported by
the LexGLUE authors themselves as a preliminary experiment (Appendix D of
https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00976) `[P]`. It is a **real-label, public benchmark with a measured
long-vs-truncated gap of the same direction and rough magnitude as ours** — and unlike our synthetic
probe it is a legally meaningful task. ECtHR is 9,000/1,000/1,000 (verified on HF), CC-BY-4.0, ~11K
cases of multi-paragraph facts. **This is the closest public analogue to our failure mode that has a
published truncated-vs-long reference gap.**

LexGLUE Table 3 (test), the paper's own run: μ-F1 / m-F1 per task. Starred tasks use the
**hierarchical** variant of each model; Longformer and BigBird are used flat (no hierarchy).

| model | ECtHR-A m-F1 | ECtHR-B m-F1 | SCOTUS m-F1 | EUR-LEX m-F1 | LEDGAR m-F1 | CaseHOLD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TFIDF+SVM | 51.7 | 65.1 | 69.5 | 51.4 | 82.4 | n/a |
| BERT (hier.) | 63.6 | 73.4 | 58.3 | 57.2 | 81.8 | 70.8 |
| RoBERTa (hier.) | 59.0 | 68.9 | 62.0 | 57.9 | 82.3 | 71.4 |
| DeBERTa (hier.) | 60.8 | 71.0 | 62.7 | 57.4 | 83.1 | 72.6 |
| **Longformer (flat, 4096)** | **64.7** | 71.7 | **64.0** | 57.7 | 83.0 | 71.9 |
| BigBird (flat, 4096) | 62.9 | 70.9 | 62.0 | 56.8 | 82.6 | 70.8 |
| Legal-BERT (hier.) | 64.0 | **74.7** | 66.5 | 57.4 | 83.0 | 75.3 |
| CaseLaw-BERT (hier.) | 62.9 | 70.3 | **65.9** | 56.6 | 83.0 | **75.4** |

`[P]` — extracted from https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00976, Table 3.

**One row of that table must not be read naively.** TF-IDF+SVM shows 69.5 m-F1 on SCOTUS, above
Longformer's 64.0, which invites the conclusion that a linear bag-of-words model beats a long-context
encoder. **LexGLUE's own v4 revision contains a correction note stating that the TF-IDF+SVM models
"are significantly outperformed in most cases, even on the SCOTUS dataset."** `[I]` — reported by
`R-003.md`, which also marks the margin `[NOT MEASURED]`. I have not independently re-run it. The
defensible reading of the LexGLUE table is therefore that **domain-adaptive pretraining dominates**
(Legal-BERT 76.4 vs Longformer 72.9 on SCOTUS μ-F1), not that sparse attention loses to TF-IDF.

**How to read this honestly, because it cuts both ways:**

- **For long-context encoders:** used *flat*, without hierarchy, Longformer beats every hierarchical
  general-domain model on ECtHR-A m-F1 (64.7 vs BERT-hier 63.6) and on SCOTUS (64.0 vs 62.7). So on
  genuinely long legal documents, a long-context sparse encoder is competitive-to-better without any
  hierarchy at all. This is the strongest positive evidence I found for the "just widen the encoder"
  family on real labels.
- **Against them:** Longformer is *not* better everywhere — it loses to Legal-BERT on ECtHR-B
  (71.7 vs 74.7) and to CaseLaw-BERT on ECtHR-A. The best ECtHR numbers come from **domain-specific
  pretraining** (Legal-BERT, CaseLaw-BERT), not from context length. And on ECtHR-A the *hierarchical*
  Legal-BERT (64.0) is statistically indistinguishable from flat Longformer (64.7) — so "context" and
  "hierarchy" are not separable from this table alone.
- **Note on EUR-LEX:** LexGLUE uses plain flat [CLS] truncation for EUR-LEX, so its 56.6–57.9 m-F1
  band is a **truncation baseline**, not a long-context result. Anyone quoting "EUR-LEX ≈ 57 m-F1" as
  a target must state that. It is also exactly the point of §5.1's first paragraph: the same dataset,
  read long instead of truncated, is where the gap appears.

**Still `UNRETRIEVED`:** a current (2024–2026) SOTA table for long-context classification on ECtHR or
EUR-LEX — in particular any ModernBERT-family result at 8192 on either. I searched for this
specifically and found none. The numbers above are from 2022, and the honest statement is that
**there is no current published number for a ModernBERT-class model doing long-context legal
classification**, which is consistent with §5's finding that the architecture's own paper has no
long-document classification evaluation.

**Corrections to IDs named in issue #3 and the brief, verified against the arXiv API `[I]`:**

- **Loong is not a classification benchmark.** arXiv 2406.17419 resolves to *"Leave No Document
  Behind: Benchmarking Long-Context LLMs with Extended Multi-Doc QA"* — a **multi-document QA**
  benchmark evaluated on decoder-only LLMs. Not usable as a labelled long-doc classification set.
- **"Found in the Middle" is two different papers.** `2403.04797` = *"Found in the Middle: How
  Language Models Use Long Contexts Better via Plug-and-Play Positional Encoding"*;
  `2406.16008` = *"Found in the Middle: Calibrating Positional Attention Bias Improves Long Context
  Utilization"*. The ID in the brief (`2406.16008`) is the second one. Both are decoder-only.
- *Lost in the Middle* is `2307.03172` (verified via arXiv API).
- **"Same Task, More Tokens" is `2402.14848`, not `2402.14809`.** `2402.14809` resolves to
  *CriticBench*. I made this error on a first guess and corrected it against the arXiv API; recorded
  here because a wrong ID in the ledger is worse than a missing one.
- **LED has no standalone arXiv entry.** `2004.11886` — which I had noted from the brief's list as
  LED — resolves to *"Lite Transformer with Long-Short Range Attention"*, an unrelated model. LED is
  defined inside the Longformer paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150). Confirmed by title search:
  the only arXiv entry matching `"Longformer-Encoder-Decoder"` is Longformer itself.

**Three of the arXiv IDs supplied in the task brief were wrong** (LED, "Same Task, More Tokens", and
the disambiguation of "Found in the Middle"), and one supplied benchmark (Loong) is QA rather than
classification. That is a 4-for-4 hit rate on the recalled specifics, which is the reason this
document resolves **every** ID through the arXiv API and states the resolution rather than the
number alone. Any downstream document that copies an ID from the brief without re-resolving it may
be citing the wrong paper.

**Position-of-evidence literature is decoder-only.** *Lost in the Middle* (`2307.03172`) and both
"Found in the Middle" papers study causal LMs on retrieval/QA. I retrieved **no** paper measuring
"position of the decisive evidence vs accuracy" for a **bidirectional encoder with a decision head**
— which is precisely our setup. See §7.

## 6. Negative results, ranked by how much they should change our design

1. **RoPE scaling has no legitimate operating point inside the trained window.** Dynamic scaling is
   *defined* as identity below L; fixed scaling below L causes "a flat reduction of performance";
   YaRN/NTK/LongRoPE cost 3.5–7.6 MMLU points when applied at short lengths; the SOTA fix is to
   switch rescaled RoPE off for inputs within the original window.
   → https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ , https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20082 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13753 `[P]`
   **Effect: rule the whole RoPE-scaling family out as a candidate for the 1024↔8192 gap.**
2. **Perplexity is not a valid readout** — the best-PPL RoPE base had the worst retrieval accuracy.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14591 `[P]`
   **Effect: our harness must score the decision task only.**
3. **In the one published window-size ablation on long-doc classification, an 8× wider window
   bought +0.4 micro-F1 for 2.5× test cost** — and that was *with retraining*.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683 `[P]`
   **Effect: bound expectations for window widening; if inference-time widening shows a large gain,
   suspect a measurement artefact before believing it.**
3b. **(corrected in place, §2.5) Inference-time window change has been measured, and widening does
   not buy accuracy.** Widening 2k→4k alone moved SWAA's LongMemEval accuracy 38.2 → 38.0;
   StreamingLLM's cache-size sweep got *worse* past a point (MPT-7B 14.12 → 14.99 monotone over
   252→2044); Gemma 2's inference-time window table only ever narrows (4096→1024, PPL
   1.63→1.64).
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10411 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00118 `[P]`
   **Effect: deprioritise window widening as a mechanism; the lever in these studies was restoring
   global attention paths, not widening the local one.**
3c. **(positive, and the sharpest single pointer we have) Restoring global attention post-hoc
   recovers the full-attention baseline with no retraining** — SWAA reaches 73.0 vs a 73.0 baseline via
   FA-layer interleaving + FA decode + keep-first-k. DuoAttention does it at *head* granularity, but
   needs a gate-optimisation pass and an 8-GPU node, and "modifying retrieval heads severely damages
   performance" while constraining streaming heads is harmless.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10411 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10819 `[P]`
   **Effect: this is the arm the prior art actually supports. Note the asymmetry — a wrong guess is
   free in one direction and catastrophic in the other, so identify load-bearing layers first.**
4. **Adding global attention did not improve F1** (Longformer), and **global-every-layer matched
   global-every-3rd-layer** (ModernBERT) — but note these are *training-time* ablations of how many
   layers are global, which is a different intervention from 3c's post-hoc restoration of global
   *paths*. Do not collapse the two.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06683 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663 `[P]`
5. **On the one benchmark built to move decisive evidence to the end of the document, a
   long-context encoder lost to a 512-token truncation baseline by ~14 micro-F1 points**, while
   chunk-and-select baselines were the best models on that column.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11258 `[P]`, third-party evaluation of the long-context methods
6. **NTK-aware interpolation is worse than plain PI after long-context fine-tuning.**
   → https://blog.eleuther.ai/yarn/ `[P]`
7. **Larger RoPE bases have a hard extrapolation upper bound and degrade faster past it than
   dynamic NTK.**
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05209 `[P]`
8. **Dropping positional encoding entirely (NoPE) beat every explicit positional encoding at length
   generalization.** Decoder-only, trained from scratch, reasoning tasks — **does not transfer**, but
   it is the strongest available signal that positional-encoding surgery is not where the wins are.
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19466 `[P]`
9. **A production ModernBERT inference path silently ignored the sliding window entirely.**
   → https://github.com/vllm-project/semantic-router/issues/3406 `[U]`
   **Effect: verify the baseline applies its own mask before attributing anything to a mask change.**
10. **The only public YaRN extension of a ModernBERT-family encoder publishes an evaluation that is
    not credible** (PPL exactly 1.00 at every length for both trained and base model).
    → https://huggingface.co/llm-semantic-router/modernbert-base-32k `[U]`
    **Effect: there is no usable worked example to copy.**

## 7. Gaps — things the literature does **not** answer for our setting

Stated as gaps so nobody mistakes silence for support.

1. **No measurement of RoPE scaling on a bidirectional encoder for a classification decision.** All
   scaling literature is causal-LM generation/perplexity/retrieval.
2. **No inference-time window *widening* on a model trained with a *narrow* window** (our mismatch
   direction). The measured cases (§2.5) all go the other way — dense-trained models run windowed, or
   narrowing. Widening *is* measured and does not help; widening *from a narrow-trained checkpoint*
   is not.
3. **No post-hoc `layer_types` swap on a bidirectional encoder checkpoint.** SWAA and DuoAttention
   (§3.2) establish the mechanism in decoder LMs; the encoder case is untested.
4. **No published sliding-window or global/local ablation for mmBERT at all.** The lineage paper
   fixes both values and never varies them.
5. **No "position of decisive evidence vs accuracy" study for encoders with a decision head.**
   *Lost in the Middle* and both *Found in the Middle* papers are decoder-only.
6. **No encoder-side analogue of RULER, and no long-document classification evaluation of the
   ModernBERT family anywhere** (§5, §5.1). The ModernBERT authors say so themselves: *"there are
   relatively few standardized long-context benchmarks for encoder-only models, and most benchmarks,
   such as Needle-in-a-haystack and RULER are geared towards generative tasks."* `[P]`
   → https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13663
7. **No current SOTA table for long-context legal classification** (ECtHR / EUR-LEX) at 8192, and no
   ModernBERT-family number on either. The newest comparable numbers I retrieved are from 2022 (§5.1).
