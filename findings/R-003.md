# R-003 — Cross-attention / hierarchical hybrids and long-document classification benchmarks: prior art

Owner: research (sub-agent B). Companion: R-001.md.

## Scope

**In scope.** Published, retrievable work on: (1) latent-bottleneck cross-attention (Perceiver / Perceiver IO);
(2) chunked-encoder + cross-attention / sparse-attention hybrids for long documents (Longformer, BigBird, LED);
(3) hierarchical / Funnel / Hourglass compression; (4) head-to-head comparisons of cheap vs. expensive long-document
classification; (5) long-document classification benchmarks with real labels, their sizes, licences and access
restrictions; (6) receptive-field and marker-token information-flow arguments, and the "position of evidence vs
accuracy" literature.

**Out of scope / explicitly deprioritised.** Generation, summarisation, retrieval-augmented QA, and document
*ranking* — these appear only where they are the *only* available evidence for a question that matters here, and
are labelled as such at the point of use.

**Constraint we are designing under** (given to me; treated as fixed input, never as a claim):
bidirectional encoder, native 8192 positions, 22 layers, hidden 768, alternating local (±64) / global attention.
Single 8 GB GPU, 15 GB RAM.

**Method and honesty rules applied throughout.**
- `web_search` was unavailable (no API key) and was not used. Discovery was via
  `https://lite.duckduckgo.com/lite/?q=…`, the arXiv API (`https://export.arxiv.org/api/query`), the arXiv
  full-text HTML, ACL Anthology PDFs (via `curl` + `pdftotext`), and the HuggingFace APIs.
- **Every arXiv ID cited below was verified this session** against `export.arxiv.org/api/query`. Several IDs I was
  given as leads were wrong and are corrected in §7.
- **Every URL cited below was fetched this session and returned HTTP 200.** A batch verification pass was run over
  the full citation list.
- Numbers are copied from the papers' **result tables**, not from prose or abstracts, except where explicitly noted.
  Where a number is only in a figure, I say so.
- `**Status:** paper-asserted` means the authors report it about their own runs. I found **very few** independent
  reproductions in this literature; where they exist they are named. Absence of independent replication is stated
  rather than glossed.
- Arithmetic I perform on cited numbers is tagged `[DERIVED]` with the formula. It is not a measurement.
- I make **no** claims about mmBERT, laya, or any in-house model. Where a cited paper happens to use mmBERT as a
  backbone, that is reported as a fact about the paper.

## 1. Perceiver / Perceiver-IO

### 1.1 Perceiver (2103.03206) does **zero** text or document classification

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03206 · https://arxiv.org/html/2103.03206v2
- **What was measured**: Jaegle, Gimeno, Brock, Zisserman, Vinyals, Carreira, "Perceiver: General Perception with
  Iterative Attention", ICML 2021. Evaluated on ImageNet, ModelNet40 (point clouds), AudioSet (audio, video,
  video+audio). The arXiv listing classifies it `cs.CV` only.
- **By whom**: authors (DeepMind).
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**. This is a *negative* relevance result, verified by reading the full text: the only
  occurrences of "language" are (a) remarks about Transformers in general, (b) the note that the latent Transformer
  borrows the GPT-2 architecture. There is no text dataset, no GLUE, no document task anywhere in the paper.
- **Implies for us**: Perceiver cannot be cited as evidence that latent-bottleneck cross-attention works for
  long-document *classification*. Its evidence base is non-textual perception.

### 1.2 Perceiver's complexity: cross-attention is O(MN); depth is decoupled from input size

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2103.03206v2 (§3, "Taming quadratic complexity with cross-attention";
  "Uncoupling depth with a latent Transformer")
- **What was measured**: analytic complexity, not runtime. Verbatim: QKV self-attention is `O(M²)`; with `Q` a
  projection of a learned latent array with index dimension `N ≪ M`, "the resulting cross-attention operation has
  complexity **O(MN)**". Latent Transformer costs `O(N²)`. Total: **`O(MN + LN²)`**, where `L` is depth.
  "…by decoupling the input size and the depth, we can add additional Transformer layers at a cost that's
  independent of the input size." Best ImageNet model: 8 cross-attends over the full 50,176-pixel input; latent
  array 512 indices × 1024 channels; 48 latent Transformer blocks; ~45M parameters.
- **By whom**: authors.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted analytic derivation**, not measured. No wall-clock or memory scaling curve is given
  as a function of `M`.
- **Implies for us**: **Yes, the cost really is linear in input length** — but the linear factor is the number of
  cross-attend layers, each of which touches every input position. The saving is not in the encoder cross-attend;
  it is that the *deep* stack operates on `N` latents instead of `M` tokens. So "becomes O(N)" is right for the
  deep stack and **wrong** for the input-facing layers, which remain `O(M·N)` each and are re-run once per
  cross-attend. For an 8192-position input this distinction is the whole ballgame.

### 1.3 Perceiver IO (2107.14795): the only language evidence is GLUE, at ≤2048 **bytes**

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.14795 · https://arxiv.org/html/2107.14795v3
- **What was measured**: Perceiver IO on the **GLUE** benchmark (§4.1, Table 1 and Table 14). Verbatim: "We
  evaluate the quality of the learned representation on the GLUE benchmark". Table 1:

  | Model | Tokenization | M (inputs) | N (latents) | Depth | Params | FLOPs | Avg. GLUE |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | BERT Base (test, from Devlin et al.) | SentencePiece | 512 | 512 | 12 | 110M | 109B | 81.0 |
  | BERT Base (ours) | SentencePiece | 512 | 512 | 12 | 110M | 109B | 81.1 |
  | Perceiver IO Base | SentencePiece | 512 | 256 | 26 | 223M | 119B | 81.2 |
  | BERT (matching FLOPs) | UTF-8 bytes | 2048 | 2048 | 6 | 20M | 130B | 71.5 |
  | Perceiver IO | UTF-8 bytes | 2048 | 256 | 26 | 201M | 113B | 81.0 |
  | Perceiver IO++ | UTF-8 bytes | 2048 | 256 | 40 | 425M | 241B | 81.8 |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **The relevance trap**: GLUE tasks are sentence/sentence-pair tasks. The byte-level model's input is
  **2048 UTF-8 bytes**, which is *shorter* than a typical long document. The paper never runs a document
  classification task and never exceeds 2048 input positions for language. So Perceiver IO's "linear scaling"
  claim is an *architectural* claim that its language experiments do not exercise on long documents.
- **Honest reading of the 71.5 vs 81.0 gap**: the "BERT (matching FLOPs)" baseline is a deliberately crippled
  20M-parameter, 6-layer model. The fair comparison is Perceiver IO (81.0) vs BERT Base (81.0/81.1), i.e. **parity,
  not a win**. Do not cite "Perceiver IO beats BERT by 9.5 points on GLUE" — that is a baseline-construction
  artefact.
- **Implies for us**: latent-bottleneck cross-attention is *competitive* with a plain encoder at short lengths under
  a matched-FLOPs budget. There is **no published evidence here that it wins at long-document classification**.

### 1.4 Latent-count ablation: 128 latents is a 5-point collapse; 256 and 512 are equivalent

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2107.14795v3, Appendix F.5, Table 15
- **What was measured**: number of latents `N` vs average GLUE score, with latent width `D` adapted to hold FLOPs
  roughly constant. **This is the latent-count ablation you asked for.**

  | N (latents) | D (latent width) | FLOPs | Avg. GLUE |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 128 | 1920 | 120B | **75.84** |
  | 256 | 1280 | 113B | **80.95** |
  | 512 | 896 | 125B | **80.92** |

- **By whom**: authors.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Implies for us**: there is a **sharp floor** below which the bottleneck destroys performance (128 latents =
  −5.1 points vs 256), and then **flat returns** (256 ≈ 512, difference 0.03 points). If we adopt a latent
  bottleneck, the hyperparameter to defend is the *floor*, not the exact value. Note this ablation is on GLUE at
  512 tokens / 2048 bytes — **it tells us nothing about whether the floor moves at 8192 positions.** That
  extrapolation is unmeasured and would be the single most informative ablation we could run.

### 1.5 Perceiver IO's O(N) claim, stated precisely

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2107.14795v3, Appendix D (FLOPs calculation) and §2
- **What was measured**: verbatim — "A model with `L` latent attention blocks has complexity **`O([M + O + L·N]·N·F)`**.
  In other words, Perceiver IO has complexity **linear in the size of the input and output arrays** and it
  **decouples the depth of the latent transformer from the input and output sizes**." Also: "Perceiver IO has no
  quadratic dependence on the input or output size: encoder and decoder attention modules depend linearly on the
  input and output size (respectively), while latent attention is independent of both."
- **Status**: **paper-asserted analytic derivation**.
- **Implies for us**: this is the cleanest published statement of *what a latent bottleneck buys*. It buys
  **depth independent of input length**. It does **not** buy a smaller per-layer input-facing cost, and it does not
  reduce the `O(M·N·F)` encoder and decoder passes, which must be paid once per cross-attend / output query.

## 2. Chunked encoding + cross-attention: the Longformer / BigBird / LED line

### 2.1 Longformer's classification gains are real, but they were obtained at RoBERTa's compute

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05150 · https://arxiv.org/html/2004.05150v2 (Beltagy, Peters, Cohan)
- **What was measured**: Longformer-base vs RoBERTa-base on the **development sets**, from Table 7. Input length
  statistics from Table 6 (wordpieces, avg / 95th percentile).

  | Dataset | Task | RoBERTa-base | Longformer-base | Δ |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | WikiHop (avg 1,535 / 95th 3,627) | acc | 72.4 | **75.0** | +2.6 |
  | TriviaQA (avg 6,589 / 95th 17,126) | F1 | 74.3 | 75.2 | +0.9 |
  | HotpotQA (avg 1,316 / 95th 1,889) | joint F1 | 63.5 | 64.4 | +0.9 |
  | OntoNotes (avg 506 / 95th 1,147) | avg F1 | 78.4 | 78.6 | +0.2 |
  | **IMDB (avg 300 / 95th 705)** | acc | 95.3 | 95.7 | **+0.4** |
  | **Hyperpartisan (avg 705 / 95th 1,975)** | F1 | 87.4 | **94.8** | **+7.4** |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Two facts that constrain the interpretation**:
  1. Verbatim §5: "We use sliding window attention with window size of **512**, therefore using the **same amount
     of computation as RoBERTa**." The finetuning-time gain therefore comes from (a) no truncation and (b) global
     attention on `[CLS]` — **not** from a wide local window.
  2. Verbatim §5 ablation (Table 10 discussion): "when configured as in RoBERTa-base (seqlen: 512, and n² attention)
     Longformer performs slightly worse than RoBERTa-base, confirming that performance gains are not due to
     additional pretraining." This is the authors' own control, and it is a strong one: it isolates the *attention
     pattern plus context length*, not extra pretraining compute.
- **IMDB is not a long-document benchmark**: avg 300 wordpieces, 95th percentile 705, and only ~13.6% of documents
  exceed 512 wordpieces. The +0.4 acc is consistent with truncation almost never biting. Treat IMDB as a
  *sanity check that long-context machinery does no harm*, not as evidence it helps.
- **Hyperpartisan is the one big win (+7.4 F1) — and it is the one dataset you should trust least.** See §5.1 and
  the UNRETRIEVED section: its test set is 65 documents, and the authors of a later study explicitly decline to draw conclusions from it.

### 2.2 Longformer's receptive-field law (this is the citable formula for §6)

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2004.05150v2, §3.1 "Sliding Window" and "Dilated Sliding Window"
- **What was measured**: analytic. Verbatim: "Given a fixed window size `w`, each token attends to **½w tokens on
  each side**… The computation complexity of this pattern is `O(n × w)`… **In a transformer with ℓ layers, the
  receptive field size at the top layer is ℓ × w** (assuming `w` is fixed for all layers)." And for dilation:
  "Assuming a fixed `d` and `w` for all layers, **the receptive field is ℓ × d × w**."
- **By whom**: authors.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted analytic derivation**. It is **not measured** — Longformer never runs an experiment
  that varies `w` or `ℓ` against a receptive-field probe.
- **Uniqueness check**: I verified that the string "receptive field" appears in **BigBird (2007.14062)** only in an
  unrelated sense, and this `ℓ × w` law is Longformer's. **Do not attribute it to BigBird.**

### 2.3 Global attention is what makes the `[CLS]` marker reachable — and it is O(n) only because it is sparse

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2004.05150v2, §3.1 "Global Attention"
- **What was measured**: verbatim: "we add 'global attention' on few pre-selected input locations. Importantly, we
  make this attention operation **symmetric**: that is, a token with a global attention attends to all tokens
  across the sequence, **and all tokens in the sequence attend to it**. … For example **for classification, global
  attention is used for the `[CLS]` token**… Since the number of such tokens is small relative to and independent
  of `n` the complexity of the combined local and global attention is still **`O(n)`**."
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Implies for us**: this is the load-bearing mechanism for any design with a decision marker at position 0. A
  local-only stack reaches the prefix only via depth (§6.1); a *single global `[CLS]`* gives every position a
  one-hop path to the marker, and every layer afterwards can mix. If the marker is to be the decision head, global
  attention on that marker is not a refinement — it is the mechanism.
- **Cost caveat**: the `O(n)` claim holds because the number of global tokens `g` is constant. Symmetric global
  attention costs `O(n·g)` extra per layer; if `g` grows with `n` (e.g. all question tokens), the constant is no
  longer constant.

### 2.4 LED is a **summarisation** result. It reports **no classification number at all.**

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2004.05150v2, §7.2 and Table 11
- **What was measured**: LED (Longformer-Encoder-Decoder), initialised from BART, on the **arXiv summarisation
  dataset** (Cohan et al.). "The 90th percentile of document lengths is **14.5K tokens**." Encoder: local window
  **1,024** tokens + global on the first `<s>`; decoder: **full attention** to the entire encoder output.
  LED-base = 6 layers, LED-large = 12 layers in each stack; position embeddings extended from BART's 1K to 16K by
  copying the 1K matrix 16 times.

  | Model | R-1 | R-2 | R-L |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Discourse-aware (2018) | 35.80 | 11.05 | 31.80 |
  | Pegasus (2020) | 44.21 | 16.95 | 38.83 |
  | **LED-large (seqlen 4,096)** | 44.40 | 17.94 | 39.76 |
  | **BigBird (seqlen 4,096)** | **46.63** | 19.02 | **41.77** |
  | **LED-large (seqlen 16,384)** | **46.63** | **19.62** | **41.83** |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **The honest negative inside Table 11**: at *equal* sequence length (4,096), **LED-large loses to BigBird on R-1
  (44.40 vs 46.63) and R-L (39.76 vs 41.77)**. LED only matches/beats BigBird once it is allowed 16,384 tokens.
  The authors attribute this to BigBird warm-starting from Pegasus. The design lesson stands regardless:
  **paying for length is what bought the number, not the architecture** — and the authors' own Fig. 3 shows ROUGE
  rising monotonically with input length.
- **Cost**: LED's decoder attends fully over the whole encoder output. That is an `O(n_enc · n_dec)` term that does
  not shrink with encoder sparsity. For **classification** this term is absent (there is no decoder), which is a
  point in favour of encoder-only designs — but it also means **LED's numbers transfer to our problem only weakly**.
- **Implies for us**: do not cite LED as long-document-classification evidence. It is a generation result on
  14.5K-token documents whose cost profile includes a full-attention decoder.

### 2.5 BigBird: the clearest published head-to-head of truncation vs. sparse attention

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.14062 · https://arxiv.org/html/2007.14062v2 (Zaheer et al.)
- **What was measured**: Table 15 — document classification, **micro-F1 on the test set**, BigBird encoder consuming
  **4,096 tokens** vs "plain RoBERTa model with **512 tokens truncation**" (their words), single seed for the large
  datasets and 5 runs for the small ones.

  | | IMDb | Yelp-5 | **Arxiv** | **Patents** | **Hyperpartisan** |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | # Examples | 25,000 | 650,000 | **30,043** | **1,890,093** | **645** |
  | # Classes | 2 | 5 | **11** | **663** | 2 |
  | Excess fraction (>512 tokens) | 0.14 | 0.04 | **1.00** | **0.90** | 0.53 |
  | Reported SoTA (non-BERT) | 97.4 | 73.28 | 87.96 | 69.01 | 90.6 |
  | RoBERTa (512 truncation) | 95.0 ± 0.2 | 71.75 | 87.42 | 67.07 | 87.8 ± 0.8 |
  | **BigBird (4,096)** | **95.2 ± 0.2** | **72.16** | **92.31** | **69.30** | **92.2 ± 1.7** |
  | Δ vs truncation | **+0.2** | +0.41 | **+4.89** | +2.23 | **+4.4** |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **This table is the single most useful artefact in the literature for our decision**, because it reports the
  per-dataset **"excess fraction"** — the share of documents that actually exceed the 512-token truncation budget —
  alongside the gain. The pattern is monotone and matches intuition:

  | Excess fraction | Dataset | Gain from 4,096 tokens |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0.04 | Yelp-5 | +0.41 |
  | 0.14 | IMDb | +0.2 |
  | 0.53 | Hyperpartisan | +4.4 |
  | 0.90 | Patents | +2.23 |
  | 1.00 | Arxiv | +4.89 |

- **Implies for us**: the expected benefit of long-context machinery is governed by **the fraction of documents
  whose label-bearing content lies beyond the truncation budget**, not by document length per se. This gives a
  cheap, decisive, pre-registered diagnostic: **measure the excess fraction of our target corpus before choosing
  an architecture.** BigBird's own words: "We see that gains of using BigBird are more significant when we have
  longer documents and fewer training examples."
- **BigBird's `[CLS]` head**: "Following BERT, we used **one layer with cross entropy loss on top of the first
  `[CLS]` token** from the BigBird encoder consuming 4096 tokens." Its global tokens are `2×block = 2b`, i.e.
  two global blocks. So the published long-document SOTA on Arxiv is a **prefix-marker head made reachable by
  global tokens**.

### 2.6 BigBird's cost: sparse attention is *not* free on GPUs

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/html/2007.14062v2, Appendix (block-sparse kernel) and the conclusion
- **What was measured**: verbatim (conclusion): "We complement these results by showing that **moving to sparse
  attention mechanism do incur a cost**." And in the kernel section: "it is well known that such sparse
  multiplications **cannot be efficiently implemented in GPUs**. GPUs have thousands of cores performing
  operations in parallel. Thus, we cannot efficiently perform the sparse matrix multiplication."
  The fix is blockification: computing the block-diagonal portion with `O(n·b·d)`, and the full attention score for
  the BigBird pattern at cost **`O(n·(g+w+r)·b·d)`** — where `g`, `w`, `r` are the global, window and random block
  counts and `b` is the block size. Parameters used for classification (Table 14): block length `b = 64`,
  `g = 2b`, `w = 3b`, `r = 3b`, max sequence length 4,096, base models 12 layers/768 hidden.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Implies for us**: the effective cost is `n × (g+w+r) × b`, i.e. linear in `n` but with a **large constant**
  (here `(2+3+3)·64 = 512` keys attended per query, matching RoBERTa-512's effective width). The sparsity buys
  *length at constant per-token width*, not a cheaper per-token operation. Random attention additionally requires
  a `gather`, which the paper notes is the one non-dense, hardware-unfriendly part.

## 3. Funnel / Hourglass hierarchies: where the pooling happens, and what it does to a marker token

### 3.1 Funnel-Transformer explicitly protects the `[cls]` token from pooling — and ablates it

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03236 · https://arxiv.org/html/2006.03236v1 (Dai, Lai, Yang, Le)
- **What was measured**: Funnel-Transformer (F-TFM) compresses the hidden sequence between blocks via **strided mean
  pooling with stride 2 and window size 2**, so a 512-token input becomes 256 then 128 across three blocks
  (layouts written `B6-6-6`). Verbatim: "for a Transformer of BERT_Base size … B6-6-6H768 … when finetuned for
  classification, the FLOPs … corresponds to at most `6+6/2+6/4 = 10.5` full-length layers, clearly fewer than
  L12H768."
- **The marker-token detail — directly on point.** Verbatim: "recall that a particular design in language
  pretraining is to add a special token `[cls]` to the beginning of the original input sequence, and use the
  last-layer hidden state corresponding to `[cls]` … as the representation of the sequence. **To prevent the pooling
  from destroying this special structure, we first separate the `[cls]` hidden state and the rest of hidden states
  and only apply the pooling to the rest of hidden states.**"
- **The ablation (Table 6, GLUE-AVG)** — this is the evidence that the protection matters, and how much:

  | ID | Layout | Pool-Op | Pool-query-only | Sep `[cls]` | Rel-Attn | GLUE-AVG |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | (1) | B6-6-6 | Mean | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | **83.5** |
  | (2) | B6-6-6 | Mean | ✓ | — | ✓ | **82.9** (−0.6) |
  | (3) | B6-6-6 | Mean | — | ✓ | ✓ | 83.0 (−0.5) |
  | (4) | B6-6-6 | Mean | ✓ | ✓ | — | 81.4 (−2.1) |
  | (5) | B6-6-6 | **Max** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | 83.4 (−0.1) |
  | (6) | B6-6-6 | **Top-Attn** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | **75.8 (−7.7)** |

- **By whom**: authors (CMU / Google).
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Two findings here, one of them a strong negative**:
  1. Protecting the `[cls]` vector from pooling is worth **+0.6 GLUE-AVG**. Small but real and cheap.
  2. **Attention-based pooling that is not pool-query-only is catastrophic: −7.7 points (83.5 → 75.8).** A
     "learned pooling" layer is not a safe default.
- **Honest limits**: F-TFM is evaluated at **512 tokens max** on GLUE, RACE and 7 short text-classification sets
  (IMDB, AG, DBpedia, Yelp-2/5, Amazon-2/5). It is **not a long-document model**, and compression is only **4×**.
  The authors also report a negative: "When we only compress the sequence length without increasing the depth (and
  #Params), F-TFM could suffer from some performance loss in certain settings on the GLUE datasets."
- **Independent follow-up (rare in this literature)**: Choi, Spangher, Hidey, Grabowski, Eskander, "Revisiting
  Funnel Transformers for Modern LLM Architectures…", **arXiv:2504.02877** (Google). They apply funneling to Gemma2
  and report: "funneling creates **information bottlenecks that propagate through deeper network layers**,
  particularly in larger models (e.g., Gemma 7B), leading to **at times unmanageable performance lost**", while a
  well-chosen funneling layer plus a good recovery operation gives "up to a **44% reduction in latency**".
  **Status**: **independently reproduced / extended by a different group** (Google, vs. the original CMU/Google
  author set — overlapping institution but a disjoint author list). This is a disconfirming-adjacent result and it
  is the closest thing to independent replication I found in the hierarchy literature.

### 3.2 Hourglass is a decoder-only LM; it reports no classification and no marker token

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13711 (Nawrot, Tworkowski, Tyrolski, Kaiser, Wu, Szegedy, Michalewski,
  "Hierarchical Transformers Are More Efficient Language Models")
- **What was measured**: enwik8 (bits-per-character) and ImageNet32/64/CIFAR-10 (bits-per-dim). **No GLUE, no
  classification, no `[CLS]` head.** Verbatim: "the model we present is **autoregressive**". It states its
  relationship to our question directly: "The first part is **analogous to the Funnel-Transformer architecture**
  (Dai et al. 2020) and the whole architecture takes inspiration from U-Nets."
- **Shortening**: "any function `S` that accepts a tensor `x` of shape `(l, d)` and returns a tensor `x'` of shape
  `(l/k, d)`, where `k` is … the *shorten factor*". Options tested: 1D average pooling (stride `k`, pool `k`),
  "linear pooling", and **attention pooling (the Funnel-style `S`)**.
- **Pooling-method ablation, Table 6** (enwik8 validation BPC, lower better; CIFAR-10 BPD):

  | Pooling method | enwik8 BPC | CIFAR-10 BPD |
  |---|---|---|
  | AvgPool | 1.129 ± 0.001 | 3.116 |
  | **Attention, S = AvgPool** | **1.124** | 3.012 |
  | Attention, S = LinearPool | 1.142 | 2.998 |
  | LinearPool | 1.159 | 2.998 |

  Verbatim: "Attention pooling reaches the lowest perplexity for both datasets. Among attention-free methods,
  average pooling performs well on text, while linear pooling works better for images."
- **Memory / throughput evidence, Table 9** (same Tesla K80 GPU, enwik8):

  | Hierarchy | BPC | Linear cost C | GB | steps/s |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 10@1 (baseline) | 1.128 | 10 | **6.99** | 0.56 |
  | **3@1 8@4 3@1** | **1.109** | 10 | **6.14** | 0.76 |
  | 12@1 (baseline) | 1.115 | 12 | **8.12** | 0.47 |
  | **4@1 8@4 4@1** | **1.098** | 12 | **7.20** | 0.62 |

  **At matched linear cost, the hierarchical model is both better (lower BPC) and cheaper in memory** — e.g. at
  C=12, 1.098 vs 1.115 BPC and 7.20 GB vs 8.12 GB.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **The decoder-specific complication, which a bidirectional encoder does not inherit**: shortening breaks causal
  masking, so Hourglass must shift the sequence right by `k−1` before every shortening: "To ensure that … we apply
  another shift right by `k−1` tokens, directly before any shortening by a factor of `k`." The paper documents a
  reduced-expressivity corner where tokens at positions divisible by `k` become unpredictable without
  fine-scale ("vanilla") layers.
- **Implies for us**: (a) the `k−1` shift and the expressivity hazard are **artefacts of autoregressiveness** and
  do not apply to a bidirectional encoder — a bidirectional chunked encoder can pool freely without an information
  leak, which removes a whole class of failure. (b) The ablation "it is beneficial to do **at least one shortening
  and by a factor of at least 3**, while keeping **2–3 vanilla layers**" is transferable design guidance. (c)
  Shrinking the local window 512 → 32 costs only **−0.6 micro-F1** (§4.4, below) — pooling and small windows are
  cheap; the deep stack is where the cost is.

### 3.3 The canonical hierarchical long-document classifier: chunk → per-chunk readout → top-level encoder

- **Source**: https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10781 (Pappagari, Żelasko, Villalba, Carmiel, Dehak, "Hierarchical
  Transformers for Long Document Classification", ASRU 2019)
- **What was measured**: segment the input into chunks, encode each with base BERT, then combine. Verbatim: "We
  segment the input into smaller chunks and feed each of them into the base model. Then, we propagate each output
  **through a single recurrent layer, or another transformer**, followed by a softmax activation. **We obtain the
  final classification decision after the last segment has been consumed.**" Models: **RoBERT** = LSTM over BERT
  chunk outputs; **ToBERT** = Transformer over BERT chunk outputs. Segments of **200 tokens, shift 50**.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**. Note it uses the *last* segment's state (an LSTM), not a prefix marker.
- **Its numbers are reproduced and extended by Dai et al. 2022** (§4.4): the "Hierarchical" model there is
  "segments … independently encoded using any pre-trained Transformer-based encoder … **We sum the contextual
  representation of the first token from each segment up with segment position embeddings as the segment
  representation** … Then the segment encoder—**two transformer blocks**—are used to capture the interaction
  between segments … By default, the aggregator is the **max-pooling** operation." **So the canonical hierarchical
  long-document classifier is a two-level design whose level-1 readout is itself a prefix-token readout.**
- **Implies for us**: the "hierarchical chunk encoder + top-level encoder" design is exactly this, and it is
  compatible with a marker-token decision head at the top level — but each chunk's summary is also taken from its
  own first token, so the design stacks two prefix readouts. §6.4 gives the measured cost of doing that.

## 4. Head-to-head: truncation vs. chunking vs. hierarchy vs. sparse attention

This is the section with the highest decision value, and it contains a genuine, unresolved **contradiction between
two peer-reviewed papers**. Both are real; they disagree; I report both and do not adjudicate.

### 4.1 The headline negative: plain 512-token truncation beats Longformer-4096 on 4 of 6 datasets

- **Source**: Park, Vyas, Shah, "Efficient Classification of Long Documents Using Transformers", ACL 2022 short,
  **arXiv:2203.11258** (ID verified) · camera-ready PDF: https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-short.79.pdf
  (I downloaded the PDF and extracted the tables with `pdftotext`; these are the camera-ready numbers.)
- **What was measured**: Table 2, test set, average over **5 seeds**. Accuracy for Hyperpartisan and 20NewsGroups;
  **micro-F1** for the rest.

  | Model | max input | Hyperpartisan (acc) | 20NewsGroups (acc) | EURLEX-57K (μF1) | Inverted EURLEX (μF1) | Book Summary (μF1) | Paired Book (μF1) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | **BERT (truncation)** | **512** | 92.00 | **84.79** | **73.09** | 70.53 | 58.18 | 52.24 |
  | BERT+TextRank | 512+512 | 91.15 | 84.99 | 72.87 | 71.30 | 58.94 | 55.99 |
  | BERT+Random | 512+512 | 89.23 | 84.65 | 73.22 | **71.47** | **59.36** | 56.58 |
  | **Longformer** | **4,096** | **95.69** | 83.39 | **54.53** | **56.47** | **56.53** | **57.76** |
  | ToBERT (hierarchical) | full (200-tok chunks) | 89.54 | 85.52 | 67.57 | 67.31 | 58.16 | 57.08 |
  | CogLTX (sentence selection) | 512 | 94.77 | 84.63 | 70.13 | 70.80 | 58.27 | 55.91 |

  Longformer **loses to 512-token truncation on 4 of 6 datasets**, and by **−18.56 μ-F1** on EURLEX-57K and
  **−14.06** on Inverted EURLEX.
- **Restricted to long documents only** (Table 5, test documents with >512 tokens) — this is the version that
  should be hardest for truncation, and the collapse gets *worse*:

  | Model | Hyperpartisan | 20NewsGroups | EURLEX | Inverted EURLEX | Book Summary | Paired Book |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | BERT | 88.00 | 86.09 | **66.76** | 62.88 | 60.56 | 52.23 |
  | BERT+TextRand | 85.63 | 85.55 | 66.56 | 64.22 | 61.76 | 56.24 |
  | BERT+Random | 83.50 | 86.18 | **67.03** | **64.31** | **62.34** | 56.77 |
  | **Longformer** | **93.17** | 85.50 | **44.66** | **47.00** | 59.66 | **58.85** |

  On documents that *all* exceed 512 tokens, truncation beats Longformer on EURLEX by **−22.10 μ-F1**.
- **Cost, Table 3** (relative to BERT, measured on Hyperpartisan): **Longformer 12.05× train time, 11.92× inference
  time, 32 GB GPU vs BERT's <16 GB.** CogLTX is **104.52× train time**. ToBERT is cheap to train (1.19×) but needs
  32 GB.
- **By whom**: authors (Amazon / Illinois).
- **Status**: **paper-asserted.** I found **no independent reproduction** of this table.

### 4.2 ⚠️ The confound that must travel with §4.1

- **Source**: https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-short.79.pdf, Appendix A (hyperparameters). Verbatim:
  > "Across all datasets, we used Adam optimizer with a learning rate of {5e-5, 3e-5, 0.005} for **one run** of
  > each model and picked the best performing learning rate for the model. **The learning rate of 0.005 was used
  > for Longformer only because it did not perform well with a learning rate of 5e-5 or 3e-5 for most of the
  > datasets.**"
- **Why this matters.** 5e-3 is ~100× the standard encoder fine-tuning rate and is a known instability regime for
  Transformer fine-tuning. Only **one run per learning rate** was done, and the model that needed the exotic rate
  is the one that collapsed. The EURLEX gap is therefore **not a clean measurement of the architecture**; an
  equally consistent explanation is that Longformer was mis-tuned at 4,096 tokens on this data.
- **Status**: the quote is **paper-asserted and verified verbatim**; the *interpretation* is mine and is flagged as
  such. **Do not cite "truncation beats Longformer by 18.6 F1 on EURLEX" as settled.** Cite it as: *a reported
  collapse whose authors document an untuned-LR caveat that plausibly explains it.*

### 4.3 Where nothing is confounded: EURLEX-57K, BERT-512 beats a full-document hierarchical model

- **Source**: Chalkidis, Fergadiotis, Malakasiotis, Androutsopoulos, "Large-Scale Multi-Label Text Classification
  on EU Legislation", ACL 2019, **arXiv:1906.02192** · https://arxiv.org/html/1906.02192v1, Table 2
- **What was measured**: 57k EU legislative documents (45k/6k/6k), ~4,271 EUROVOC labels. Table 2 caption, verbatim:
  "**Starred methods use the first 512 document tokens; all other methods use full documents.**"

  | Method | micro-F1 (all) | frequent | few-shot |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Logistic Regression (tf-idf, n=1..5), **full doc** | 0.539 | 0.767 | 0.781 |
  | **HAN (hierarchical attention), full doc** | 0.680 | 0.789 | 0.805 |
  | **bert-base \* (first 512 tokens)** | **0.732** | **0.835** | **0.846** |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**. Unlike §4.1, **this comparison has no visible LR asymmetry**: both models are the
  authors' own, tuned in the same study, and the caption is explicit that the starred model sees only the first 512
  tokens while HAN sees the whole document. **BERT-512 wins by +5.2 μ-F1 over a full-document hierarchical model.**
- **The authors' own explanation, verbatim**: they "find that considering only particular zones of the documents is
  sufficient. **This allows us to bypass BERT's maximum text length limit and fine-tune BERT, obtaining the best
  results** in all but zero-shot learning cases." And, from the later study: "Chalkidis et al. (2019) observe that
  processing the first two sections only (header and recitals) results in almost the same performance as the full
  documents and that **BERT on the first 512 tokens outperforms all the other models they considered**."
- **Implies for us**: this is the **cleanest published evidence that a simple head-truncation baseline can beat a
  full-document hierarchical model**, and it is the result I would trust most in this section. It also identifies
  the mechanism: **EURLEX documents carry their labels in the header and recitals, i.e. at the front.** That is a
  property of the corpus, and it is precisely the property that makes a prefix marker token work.

### 4.4 The direct rebuttal: on the same task family, long context wins by 13–15 F1

- **Source**: Dai, Chalkidis, Darkner, Elliott, "Revisiting Transformer-based Models for Long Document
  Classification", Findings of EMNLP 2022, **arXiv:2204.06683** (ID verified) ·
  https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.534.pdf (PDF extracted with `pdftotext`)
- **What was measured**: TrLDC = sparse-attention (Longformer) vs hierarchical, on four datasets, **max sequence
  length 4,096**, hardware Quadro RTX 6000 (24 GB) or Tesla V100 (32 GB). Baseline 512-token strategies: **First**,
  **Random**, **Informative** (TF-IDF-selected).
- **Table 2 — MIMIC-III test set, micro-F1** (50 ICD-9 codes). This is the direct counterexample:

  | Model | Micro-F1 | Micro-AUC |
  |---|---|---|
  | First (512) | 47.0 ± 0.4 | 86.0 ± 0.1 |
  | Random (512) | 42.7 ± 0.4 | 85.4 ± 0.1 |
  | Informative (512) | 46.4 ± 0.5 | 85.8 ± 0.1 |
  | **Longformer (4096 + LWAN)** | **60.7 ± 0.6** | 92.6 ± 0.2 |
  | **Hierarchical (4096 + LWAN)** | **62.9 ± 0.1** | 93.6 ± 0.0 |
  | Hierarchical (4096 + LWAN + L*) | **65.2 ± 0.2** | 94.1 ± 0.0 |
  | Hierarchical (8192 + LWAN + L*) | **65.5 ± 0.7** | 94.1 ± 0.2 |

  **+13.7 to +18.5 micro-F1 over the best 512-token baseline.** (LWAN = label-wise attention network; L* = a
  domain-specific RoBERTa-Large.)
- **Table 3 — ECtHR / 20 News / Hyperpartisan**:

  | Model | ECtHR (μF1) | 20 News (acc) | Hyperpartisan (acc) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | First (512) | 73.5 ± 0.2 | 86.1 ± 0.3 | 92.9 ± 3.2 |
  | Random (512) | **79.0 ± 0.6** | 85.3 ± 0.4 | 88.9 ± 2.5 |
  | Informative (512) | 72.4 ± 0.2 | 86.2 ± 0.3 | 91.7 ± 3.2 |
  | Longformer (4096) | 81.0 ± 0.5 | 86.3 ± 0.5 | **97.9 ± 0.7** |
  | Hierarchical (4096) | **81.1 ± 0.2** | 86.3 ± 0.2 | 95.4 ± 1.3 |

- **By whom**: authors (CSIRO Data61 / Copenhagen).
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Their explicit scope warning about Hyperpartisan** (footnote 9), verbatim: "Although Hyperpartisan is a widely
  used benchmark for long document models, **we do not recommend drawing practical conclusions based on our results
  because we observe high variance when we run experiments using different GPUs or CUDA versions.** We attribute
  this may to the small size (65) of its test set and the subjectivity of the task." **This is a direct warning
  against the one dataset on which Park et al.'s Longformer looked best (95.69).**
- **Their practical advice, verbatim** — this is the most quotable design guidance in the literature:
  - Q1: "We suggest using TrLDC models if you work with datasets consisting of long documents (e.g., **2K tokens on
    average**). We notice that on 20 News dataset, the gap between baselines that process 512 tokens and long
    document models is **negligible**."
  - Q2: "We suggest **Longformer as the starting point** if you do not plan on extensively tuning hyperparameters.
    We find the default config of Longformer is robust, although it is possible to set a **moderate size (64–128) of
    local attention window** to improve efficiency without sacrificing effectiveness, and a small number of
    additional global attention tokens to make the training more stable. On the other hand, hierarchical
    Transformers may benefit from careful hyperparameter tuning… We suggest **splitting a document into small
    non-structure-derived segments (e.g., 128 tokens) which overlap** as a starting point."
  - Their conclusion: "Taken together, these experiments **rebut the criticisms of Transformers for long document
    classification**."

### 4.5 Local window size: 512 → 128 costs 0.2 F1 and buys 2.1× speed

- **Source**: https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.534.pdf, Table 1 and Table 11 (MIMIC-III **dev** set)
- **What was measured**: Longformer local attention window size vs micro-F1 and throughput ("processed samples per
  second"; parenthesised = relative speedup).

  | Window | Micro-F1 (dev) | Throughput | Speedup |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 128 | 68.3 ± 0.3 | 7.4 | **2.1×** |
  | 256 | 68.4 ± 0.3 | 5.5 | 1.6× |
  | 512 | 68.5 ± 0.3 | 3.5 | 1.0× |
- **Also negative, and useful**: "Longformer local window can shrink 512→32 for only **−0.6 micro-F1** on MIMIC dev
  with a **2.9×** speedup" (their Figure 6 / Table 11 discussion). And: "Adding global tokens beyond `[CLS]`
  **does not improve F1**", only stabilises training; **position-based** global tokens beat **TF-IDF-content-based**
  ones. Paragraph/structure-based splitting **did not help** on ECtHR and "substantially underperformed" even
  splitting on MIMIC-III.
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Implies for us**: the local window is a **cheap** knob. A ±64 window (total 128) is in the region where the
  published cost is ~0.2 F1 relative to w=512 while doubling throughput. The expensive part is depth and the
  global/input-facing attention, not the window.

### 4.6 The 8 GB fit question, answered with published memory numbers

- **Source**: https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.534.pdf, Table 4. "We use **batch size of 2** in these
  experiments, and measure the impact of attention window size and segment length on the memory footprint."
  Longformer 148.6M params; Hierarchical 139.0M params.

  | Max seq len | Size | **Longformer** | **Hierarchical** |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1,024 | 64 | **4.8 G** | **3.6 G** |
  | 1,024 | 128 | 5.0 G | 3.8 G |
  | 1,024 | 256 | 5.5 G | 4.1 G |
  | 1,024 | 512 | 6.6 G | 4.7 G |
  | **4,096** | **64** | **11.8 G** | **7.8 G** |
  | 4,096 | 128 | 12.8 G | 8.4 G |
  | 4,096 | 256 | 14.9 G | 9.6 G |
  | 4,096 | 512 | **19.4 G** | **12.2 G** |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**, batch size 2, base-sized models.
- **Implies for us, stated plainly**: at 4,096 tokens and batch size 2, **the *cheapest* published Longformer
  configuration (11.8 GB) does not fit an 8 GB GPU**, whereas **the cheapest hierarchical configuration (7.8 GB at
  segment length 64) marginally does.** At 1,024 tokens both fit comfortably. The authors' own summary: "We find
  that **Hierarchical models require less GPU memory than Longformer in general**, and it is possible to set smaller
  local window size in Longformer or segment length in hierarchical models to fit the model using smaller GPU
  memory." **This is the single most directly actionable number in R-003 for an 8 GB budget.** Caveat: it is
  base-sized (12 layers / 768 hidden) at 4,096 tokens, not 22 layers at 8,192; our configuration is deeper and
  longer, so memory would be higher, not lower.

### 4.7 Truncation *strategy* is itself unstable — head-truncation is not a safe default

- **Source**: https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.534.pdf (Table 3 discussion)
- **What was measured**: on ECtHR, **First-512 = 73.5 vs Random-512 = 79.0** — random beats head by **+5.5** μ-F1,
  and Informative-512 = 72.4 is the worst of the three. Their words: "there is **no single best strategy** to choose
  which 512 tokens to process. Using the first 512 tokens works well on MIMIC-III and Hyperpartisan datasets, but it
  **performs much worse than 512 random tokens on ECtHR**."
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Contrast with Chalkidis et al. 2019** (§4.3), where first-512 is *optimal* on EURLEX-57K because labels live in
  the header/recitals. **Both are true. Head-truncation's quality is a property of where the label evidence sits in
  the corpus, and that differs by corpus.** Any argument that a prefix marker token suffices must be made
  per-corpus, not in general.

### 4.8 Sun et al.: head+tail is real, but its margin over plain head-only is ~0.2 error points

- **Source**: Sun, Qiu, Xu, Huang, "How to Fine-Tune BERT for Text Classification?", **arXiv:1905.05583** ·
  https://arxiv.org/html/1905.05583v3, Table 2. Test **error rate %** (lower is better). head+tail = "the first
  **128** and the last **382** tokens" (= 510).
- I read this table directly from the arXiv HTML; the numbers match those independently retrieved by a sibling
  sub-agent.

  | Method | IMDb | Sogou |
  |---|---|---|
  | head-only (first 510) | 5.63 | 2.58 |
  | tail-only (last 510) | 5.44 | 3.17 |
  | **head+tail (128 + 382)** | **5.42** | **2.43** |
  | hier. mean pooling over chunk `[CLS]` | 5.89 | 2.83 |
  | hier. max pooling over chunk `[CLS]` | 5.71 | 2.47 |
  | hier. self-attention over chunk `[CLS]` | 5.49 | 2.65 |

- **Status**: **paper-asserted**.
- **Two negatives worth carrying**:
  1. **head+tail beats plain head-only by only 0.21 (IMDb) and 0.15 (Sogou) error points.** It is the best method
     here, but the margin that made it a standard recipe is tiny.
  2. **All three hierarchical variants consumed the entire document while truncation consumed only 510 tokens, and
     truncation still won on IMDb (5.42 vs 5.49).** More tokens did not buy accuracy on this task.
- **Also**: there is **no clean primacy at 512 tokens** in this table — head-only beats tail-only on Sogou
  (2.58 vs 3.17) but *loses* on IMDb (5.63 vs 5.44). The front-vs-back ranking is dataset-dependent even for a
  classical sentiment task.
- **Caveat on the comparison**: this is mean pooling over *chunk-level `[CLS]` vectors* (a late-fusion head), which
  is **not** the same operator as token-level mean pooling in §6.4. Do not conflate them.

### 4.9 Document *ranking* (out of primary scope, but the closest thing to a mechanism study)

- **Source**: Boytsov, Lin, Gao, Zhao, Huang, Nyberg, "Understanding Performance of Long-Document Ranking Models
  through Comprehensive Evaluation and Leaderboarding", **arXiv:2207.01262** ·
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.01262v1 (2-page extended abstract; PDF extracted with `pdftotext`)
- **Scope warning**: this is **information retrieval / ranking** on MS MARCO and Robust04, with MRR / nDCG metrics
  and **no classification labels**. It is included only because it is the only work I found that *explains* why
  truncation is hard to beat, with an analysis rather than an assertion.
- **What was measured / claimed**, verbatim:
  - "Somewhat surprisingly, we find the simple **FirstP baseline** (truncating documents to satisfy the
    input-sequence constraint of a typical Transformer model) to be **quite effective**."
  - "The **FirstP (ELECTRA) baseline outstrips nearly all long-document models that use the original BERT**."
  - "…a good performance of Longformer **can be largely explained by better pre-training** compared to the original
    BERT model **rather than to its ability to effectively encode long documents**."
  - "…for MS MARCO data this happens because **the distribution of relevant passages is skewed greatly towards the
    beginning of the document**."
  - LongP models are "only **modestly better (by 5-6%)** than respective FirstP baselines"; "Longformer is also the
    slowest model to train and evaluate (about **1.5× slower** compared to PARADE Attn model)"; "training took from
    **6 hours for BERT FirstP to 24 hours for Longformer**."
- **By whom**: authors (Bosch Center for AI / CMU).
- **Status**: **paper-asserted**. No independent reproduction found.
- **Implies for us**: it supplies the **mechanism** that §4.3 and §4.7 imply empirically — *truncation wins wherever
  the label-bearing evidence is front-loaded*, and long-context models then inherit their apparent gains largely
  from better pretraining rather than from length. This is the cleanest statement of the confound to control for:
  **if you compare a long-context model to a truncated baseline, you must equalise the pretraining, not just the
  token budget.** Nearly every head-to-head table in §2–§4 fails this test.

### 4.10 Independent reproduction attempts found (both disconfirming)

- **CogLTX, re-run by Park et al.**: verbatim (camera-ready PDF): "CogLTX reported their accuracy at **87.00%** on
  the test set and **87.40%** on the long documents in the test set… Our implementation of CogLTX in the same
  setting with five different runs resulted in a much lower performance at **85.15%** on the test set and **86.57%**
  on the long documents only." **Status: independently reproduced (by Park et al., a different group) and the
  original number is not reproduced.**
- **ToBERT, re-run by Park et al.**: "we were **unable to replicate** ToBERT results on 20NewsGroups." (Table 5
  reports "–" for ToBERT on 20NewsGroups.) **Status: attempted independent reproduction, failed.**

Both are worth stating explicitly: in this sub-literature, **the two independent re-runs I found both failed to
reproduce the original numbers.**

### 4.11 Structural gap: the comparison we would most want does not exist

I could not retrieve **any** paper that compares, on the same benchmark and at matched token budgets:
head+tail truncation (Sun et al.) vs. chunk-pooling vs. hierarchical vs. sparse attention. Park et al. use head-only
512; Dai et al. use first/random/informative 512 but never head+tail; BigBird compares only to RoBERTa-512;
LexGLUE compares hierarchical-at-512 vs flat-at-4096 but not against truncation variants. Also, **no paper I could
retrieve equalises FLOPs between a 512-truncation baseline and a 4,096 sparse-attention model.** The "cheap vs
expensive" question is therefore, as of this reading, **not settled in the literature** — and R-003's most useful
output is the list of confounds to avoid, not a winner.

## 6. Receptive field and marker-token information flow

### 6.1 The analytic argument, and its exact source

- **Source**: Longformer, https://arxiv.org/html/2004.05150v2, §3.1. Verbatim: *"each token attends to **½w tokens
  on each side** … **In a transformer with ℓ layers, the receptive field size at the top layer is ℓ × w**."* With
  dilation: *"the receptive field is **ℓ × d × w**."*
- **Status**: **paper-asserted analytic derivation, not measured.** Longformer never empirically probes the realised
  receptive field as a function of `w` and `ℓ`.
- **Uniqueness**: this is the **only** citable source I found for the law. The phrase "receptive field" does not
  appear in BigBird (2007.14062) in this sense. Do not attribute the formula to BigBird or ETC.

### 6.2 `[DERIVED]` arithmetic applied to the stated constraint — flagged as arithmetic, not measurement

Applying Longformer's `ℓ × w` law (per-direction reach = `ℓ · w / 2`, since each layer extends `w/2` per side) to the
given architecture (L = 22 layers, local window ±64 ⇒ w = 128):

```
per-direction local reach  =  L · w / 2  =  22 × 128 / 2  =  1,408 tokens      [DERIVED]
sequence length                                                    8,192 tokens
```

**This is arithmetic on a cited formula and given architecture parameters. It is not a measurement, and it is not
a claim about any model's behaviour.**

**What it means, kept bounded**: if *all* 22 layers were local with a ±64 window, a marker token at position 0 would
be **outside the receptive field of roughly the last ~6,800 positions** — the local stack alone cannot carry
information from late document positions back to a prefix marker. With **alternating global** layers the picture
changes qualitatively, not incrementally: a global layer gives every position a direct one-hop path to (and from)
the marker, so the `ℓ · w / 2` bound applies only to the runs of consecutive local layers between global layers.
**The number that actually governs marker reachability is therefore the length of the longest run of consecutive
local layers**, not the total depth. That is a design parameter worth naming explicitly, and I found **no published
measurement** of it.

**Gap, stated plainly**: I could not retrieve any paper that *empirically measures* the effective receptive field of
a stacked **local-attention text encoder** as a joint function of depth and window size. A sibling sub-agent ran
arXiv abstract searches `"receptive field" AND "local attention"` (36 hits, all computer vision) and
`"effective receptive field" AND "attention"` (46 hits, all CV/medical/point-cloud). The nearest empirical analogue
is a **vision** result — Raghu et al., "Do Vision Transformers See Like Convolutional Neural Networks?",
**arXiv:2108.08810** — which measures attention distance per head per layer and finds "the lower layers attend both
locally and globally, while the higher layers attend entirely globally". That is a **methodological template**
(measure attention distance vs depth), not confirmation of the `ℓ × w` law, and it uses global rather than windowed
attention.

### 6.3 Is the evidence-position literature encoder-side or decoder-only? **It is overwhelmingly decoder-only.**

This is the explicit question, so here is the explicit answer, per architecture.

**Decoder-only — where essentially all of it lives.**

- **Lost in the Middle** — Liu, Lin, Hewitt, Paranjape, Bevilacqua, Petroni, Liang, TACL 2023,
  **arXiv:2307.03172** (ID verified) · https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03172v3.
  - **What was measured**: multi-document QA (NaturalQuestions-Open, 2,655 queries; exactly 1 of *k* passages
    contains the answer; vary *k* = 10/20/30 and the gold passage's index) and synthetic key–value retrieval
    (*k* = 75/140/300 UUID pairs). **Both are retrieval-style, exact-match tasks.** Models: MPT-30B-Instruct,
    LongChat-13B-16K, GPT-3.5-Turbo(-16K), Claude-1.3(-100K), GPT-4 (subset), Llama-2 7B/13B/70B.
  - **The curve**, Fig. 1 caption, verbatim: "Changing the location of relevant information … results in a
    **U-shaped performance curve**—models are better at using relevant information that occurs at the very
    beginning (primacy bias) or end of its input context (recency bias), and performance degrades significantly
    when models must access and use information located in the middle."
  - **⚠️ Scope, verbatim from §4.1**: "**The open models we evaluated are all decoder-only models**—at each
    timestep, they may only attend to prior tokens."
  - **The one non-decoder-only result, and it is encoder-decoder, not encoder-only**: Flan-UL2 "within its
    2048-token training-time context window … its performance is relatively robust to changes in the position of
    relevant information (**1.9% absolute difference between best- and worst-case performance**)", but beyond
    2048 tokens "performance begins to degrade when relevant information is placed in the middle". The authors
    hypothesise this is because "their **bidirectional encoder** allows processing each document in the context of
    future documents" — but they flag it as a hypothesis, and their own §4.2 follow-up test **failed**.
  - **Critical caveat for our purposes**: in Flan-T5/UL2 the **decoder is still causal**; only the input encoder is
    bidirectional. So even this result is *not* evidence about a bidirectional encoder-only model with a prefix
    marker. **Lost in the Middle contains no encoder-only (BERT-style) result at all.**
  - **Status**: **paper-asserted**. Independently reproduced *as a phenomenon* by Hsieh et al. (§ below) on a
    different model.
- **Found in the Middle** — Hsieh, Chuang, Li, Wang, Le, Kumar, Glass, Ratner, Lee, Krishna, Pfister,
  ACL Findings 2024, **arXiv:2406.16008** (ID verified, matches the one given to me) ·
  https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16008v2. *(Note: an unrelated "Found in the Middle" on positional encoding exists at
  arXiv:2403.04797 — do not conflate.)*
  - **What it actually is**: a *mechanism + calibration* paper, not a benchmark paper. It establishes that the
    U-shape is an **attention bias** independent of content — "This U-shaped pattern persists even after randomly
    shuffling document order … suggesting that this bias does not depend on the documents' actual content" — and
    then subtracts an estimated bias term.
  - **The reframing that matters most for our design**, verbatim: "This finding challenges the recent belief that
    LLMs struggle to capture relevant context embedded in the middle of inputs, suggesting they **may indeed be
    capable of doing so, but are only hindered by the overwhelming positional bias**." I.e. **the middle is attended
    to; it is out-competed.**
  - **Numbers**: Table 1 (Vicuna-7b-v1.5-16k, K=20, gold at position 10): most-likely-used document falls in the
    highest-attention half in **526 examples (74%)** vs lowest half **186 (26%)**. End-to-end gains "up to **15
    percentage points**" on NaturalQuestions.
  - **Scope**: **decoder-only** (Vicuna-7b). The paper states LitM "has been observed across multiple decoder-only
    LLMs". **No encoder-only experiment.**
  - **Status**: **paper-asserted**; **no independent reproduction of the calibration method or the 74%/26% split
    found.** It does independently reproduce the LitM *phenomenon* (it re-ran it on Vicuna-7b).
  - **Its own stated limitation**: "The root cause of attention bias is unclear."

**Bidirectional-encoder-side — what exists, and what it is *not*.**

There is **no** "Lost in the Middle for encoders". The nearest published work measures *embedding similarity*,
*attention mass*, or *pooling-head accuracy* — **not** evidence-position vs. task accuracy. Being precise about that
distinction is the whole point:

1. **Quantifying Positional Biases in Text Embedding Models** — Lee, Goel, Ramchandran, **arXiv:2412.15241** ·
   https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15241v4. Scope is explicitly bidirectional encoders ("Embedding models … employ
   layers of bidirectional self-attention blocks"), and the paper notes "pooling operations are position-invariant,
   theoretically suggesting an unbiased treatment of input positions" — **and then shows that this invariance does
   not hold empirically.** Numbers: inserting irrelevant text at the start vs the end reduces cosine similarity by
   "**up to 12.3% more**" at the start; removing half the sentences from the start lowers median similarity
   **10.6%** more than removing from the end, while removing 50% from the middle leaves median similarity **95%**.
   Regression on reconstructed embeddings gives negative position coefficients for APE, RoPE and ALiBi. Crucially,
   **they repeat with randomly shuffled sentences and get the same front-loading**, so it is not an artefact of
   human writing order. **Status: paper-asserted.** **What it does not measure**: cosine similarity between
   embeddings, not task accuracy, and there is no decision head — so it demonstrates positional *sensitivity*, not
   positional *accuracy loss*.
2. **Dwell in the Beginning** — Coelho, Martins, Magalhães, Callan, Xiong, **arXiv:2404.04163** (⚠️ *not*
   2404.01261, which is FABLES). Encoder-decoder representation model on MS-MARCO; the abstract reports that "after
   contrastive pre-training the model already generates embeddings that better capture early contents of the input,
   with fine-tuning further aggravating this effect." **Status: abstract-level read only — all numbers UNRETRIEVED.**
3. **Positional Bias in Multimodal Embedding Models** — Wu, Albreiki, AAAI 2026, **arXiv:2511.11216** ·
   https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11216v1. Finds "**text encoders tend to exhibit bias toward the beginning** of the
   input, whereas image encoders show bias at both the beginning and end." **This is a primacy-only shape for text
   encoders, distinct from the decoder's primacy+recency U.** **Status: paper-asserted.**
4. **Impact of Position Bias on Language Models in Token Classification** — Ben Amor, Granitzer, Mitrović,
   **arXiv:2304.13567**. Explicitly encoder-side: "an often-overlooked issue of **encoder models**, specifically the
   position bias of **positive examples** in token classification tasks"; reports "an average drop ranging from
   **3% to 9%**". **Status: abstract-level read; per-task numbers UNRETRIEVED.** Note this is position of positive
   *examples in training data*, not evidence position in a long document.
5. **Look at the First Sentence: Position Bias in Question Answering** — Ko, Lee, Kim, Kim, Kang, EMNLP 2020,
   **arXiv:2004.14602**. **The closest thing to encoder-side evidence-position analysis**: it studies **BERT**
   (bidirectional) and "thoroughly examine[s] how position bias propagates through each layer of BERT", recovering
   BERT "from **37.48% to 81.64%**" on a deliberately position-biased SQuAD. **Status: abstract-level read; the
   layer-wise propagation analysis is UNRETRIEVED.** Caveat: this is a training-data *prior* artefact, not a
   long-document evidence-position curve.

**The classification-marker literature specifically.**

- **What Does BERT Look At?** — Clark, Khandelwal, Levy, Manning, **arXiv:1906.04341**. Reports that "**Early heads
  attend to `[CLS]`, middle heads attend to `[SEP]`, and deep heads attend to periods and commas**", and that the
  last layer's attention from `[CLS]` has "high entropy … of **3.89 nats**, indicating very broad attention … it
  attends broadly to aggregate a representation for the whole input". **Scope: BERT-base on ≤128-token Wikipedia
  segments — nothing length-dependent. Do not over-read this as a long-context result.**
- **Revealing the Dark Secrets of BERT** — Kovaleva, Romanov, Rogers, Rumshisky, **arXiv:1908.08593**. Finds a
  recurring "Vertical" attention pattern "associated predominantly, if not exclusively, with attention to `[CLS]`
  and `[SEP]`", with "a clear tendency for earlier layers to pay attention to `[CLS]` and for later layers to
  `[SEP]`". **Sobering negative, verbatim**: "we believe that the striped attention maps generally come from BERT
  **pre-training tasks rather than from task-specific linguistic reasoning**." **Scope: GLUE at BERT's 512-token
  limit.**

### 6.4 The one paper that *does* measure a prefix head vs. pooling at long length — and it uses mmBERT

- **Source**: Doshi, Trivedi, Kumar, Awasthy, Li, Sen, Florian, Joshi (IBM Research), "**LMK > CLS: Landmark
  Pooling for Dense Embeddings**", **arXiv:2601.21525** (ID verified) · https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21525v1
- **Scope**: **bidirectional encoders, at length.** I verified from the HTML that the named backbones include
  **`mmBERT-base`**, **`ModernBERT-base`**, `gte-en-mlm-base` and `gte-multilingual-base`. *(Reported as a fact about
  this paper. I make no claim about mmBERT itself.)* Benchmarks: MSMARCO, BEIR(15), MTEBv2(10), MIRACL(18),
  MLDR(8/13), COIR(6), LongEmbed(6), and **zero-shot multilingual long-document classification on Multi-EURLEX
  (23 languages), Macro-F1**.
- **The mechanism, verbatim (§3.1)** — this is the analytic statement of late→early flow for a prefix head:
  > "**Long-term decay for CLS**: The rotation matrices in RoPE induce a relative distance-based attenuation of the
  > attention logit … for large relative offsets. Specifically, for a `[CLS]` token at position m=0: … **whose
  > magnitude decreases as n increases**. After the softmax operation, this results in smaller attention weights for
  > distant tokens, **biasing the `[CLS]` representation toward early positions**. This effect becomes more
  > pronounced with longer sequences, limiting the effectiveness of `[CLS]` pooling for long-context extrapolation."
- **Empirical confirmation (their Fig. 2, described in text)**: analysing final-layer attention to the pooling token
  across long documents (>8,192 tokens from MLDR), they "observe a distinct concentration of attention toward early
  tokens in the sequence. **Fine-tuning on longer sequences reduces this bias but does not eliminate it. The effect
  worsens significantly when extrapolating beyond the training sequence length.**"

  1. **On short context, `[CLS]` is fine — it is the best or tied-best pooling on every short benchmark**

     LongEmbed 43.3 vs **62.7** vs 62.4. On the three long-context benchmarks averaged, CLS 37.2 → Mean 44.9 →
     LMK 48.1.

     worse than plain CLS. Adding marker tokens is not automatically helpful.

  effect, not an intrinsic ceiling.** In their long-context fine-tuning MSL ablation, CLS on MLDR(13) goes from

  setting, Mean is **worse** than CLS on MLDR (**17.0 vs 24.9**), so "mean pooling beats `[CLS]` at length" is
  **benchmark-dependent, not universal.**

  framing of the trade-off is worth quoting: "`[CLS]` tends to **concentrate information toward the initial
  positions** of the sequence and can **under-represent distributed evidence**, while mean pooling can dilute
  salient local signals, sometimes leading to worse short-context performance." And on `[CLS]`: it "places a heavy
  representational burden on a single position and, as we observe, often **fails to generalize effectively to long
  contexts**."

### 6.5 What this section licenses, and what it does not

**Licensed.** (a) A prefix/marker decision head in a long-context encoder is a *known-risk* design: there is a
published mechanism (RoPE long-term decay attenuating `[CLS]`→distant-token attention) and published measurements
showing prefix-pooled representations concentrate on early positions and lose ~19 points to mean pooling on
long-context retrieval, while being *fine* at short lengths. (b) Training length vs. evaluation length mismatch is a
large part of the effect and is partially fixable by fine-tuning at the evaluation length. (c) In a purely local
stack the receptive-field argument is decisive, and Longformer's `ℓ · w` law is the citable form of it.

**Not licensed.** (d) There is **no** published accuracy-vs-evidence-position curve for any bidirectional
encoder-only model, and none for Longformer, BigBird, or any hierarchical encoder — I searched and found none, and a
sibling sub-agent independently searched and found none. In particular, **there is no evidence that a prefix marker
in a bidirectional *classification* encoder exhibits a "lost in the middle" U-shape**, because the U-shape has only
ever been measured on causal decoders doing retrieval-style QA. (e) The encoder-side evidence is about *embedding
similarity*, *attention mass*, and *retrieval* — **not** about label accuracy in document classification. Anyone
citing LitM to predict classification accuracy at 8192 tokens is extrapolating across task, head, and architecture
simultaneously. That extrapolation is the honest state of the art.

## Negative results (prominent)

Collected here because they are the most decision-relevant content in R-003.

1. **Perceiver (2103.03206) contains no text classification whatsoever** — its evidence is images, point clouds,
   audio, video. Verified by reading the full text.
2. **Perceiver IO's language evidence stops at 2,048 UTF-8 bytes**, which is shorter than a long document. Its
   "linear scaling" is an architectural claim its experiments never exercise at document scale. And its apparent
   GLUE win is against a deliberately crippled 20M/6-layer byte-level BERT; against a real BERT-Base it is parity
   (81.0 vs 81.0/81.1).
3. **128 latents collapse Perceiver IO by 5.1 GLUE points** (75.84 vs 80.95 at 256); 256 and 512 are equivalent.
   The bottleneck has a floor, and the floor was never located at long lengths.
4. **LED loses to BigBird at equal sequence length** on arXiv summarisation (R-1 44.40 vs 46.63 at 4,096). Its win
   required paying for 16,384 tokens.
5. **LED reports no classification result at all.** Its decoder attends fully over the encoder output, so its cost
   profile does not transfer to encoder-only classification.
6. **Truncation beat Longformer on 4 of 6 datasets** in Park et al. (ACL 2022), by −18.56 μ-F1 on EURLEX-57K and
   −14.06 on Inverted EURLEX; and by −22.10 on the long-documents-only subset. **Caveat: the paper used a single
   run per LR and an anomalous 0.005 LR for Longformer only.**
7. **BigBird's gains vanish when documents are short**: +0.2 μ-F1 on IMDb (14% excess) and +0.41 on Yelp-5 (4%
   excess), versus +4.89 on Arxiv (100% excess). Long-context machinery is wasted spend when the excess fraction is
   low.
8. **Two independent re-runs in this literature both failed**: Park et al. could not reproduce CogLTX's 87.00%
   (got 85.15%) and could not replicate ToBERT on 20NewsGroups at all.
9. **Attention-based pooling that is not pool-query-only cost 7.7 GLUE points** in Funnel-Transformer (83.5 →
   75.8). "Learned pooling" is not a safe default.
10. **Compression without added depth hurts**: Funnel's authors report performance loss on GLUE when sequence length
    is compressed without re-investing the FLOPs in more layers.
11. **Funneling in modern LLMs creates information bottlenecks that propagate to deeper layers**, "at times
    unmanageable performance lost" in Gemma 7B (arXiv:2504.02877, independent follow-up).
12. **Naive multi-`[CLS]` pooling collapses on long retrieval**: MLDR 5.1 vs 24.9 for a single `[CLS]`
    (arXiv:2601.21525).
13. **Mean pooling is not uniformly better than `[CLS]` at length**: in the multilingual setting it is *worse* on
    MLDR (17.0 vs 24.9). And on LongEmbed's Passkey task `[CLS]` wins (82.8 vs 75.8).
14. **Head+tail truncation's advantage over plain head-only is ~0.2 error points** (5.63 → 5.42 IMDb; 2.58 → 2.43
    Sogou, arXiv:1905.05583).
15. **All three hierarchical variants lost to plain truncation on IMDb** despite consuming the entire document
    (arXiv:1905.05583).
16. **Hierarchical models are not reliably better than flat ones**: Dai et al. find Longformer and Hierarchical
    essentially tied on ECtHR (81.0 vs 81.1) and 20 News (86.3 vs 86.3).
17. **Truncation strategy choice is unstable across corpora**: First-512 73.5 vs Random-512 79.0 on ECtHR — a 5.5
    point swing from the *choice of which 512 tokens*, with no single best strategy.
18. **Structure/paragraph-based document splitting did not help** (Dai et al.), and the optimal segment length is
    dataset-dependent with no universal value.
19. **Adding global attention tokens beyond `[CLS]` did not improve F1**, only training stability.
20. **Hyperpartisan — the benchmark on which long-context models look best — has a 65-document test set and
    documented high variance across GPUs and CUDA versions**; its own users recommend against drawing conclusions
    from it.
21. **BigBird's sparse attention is slower per unit work on GPUs**, requires blockification, and its random
    component needs a `gather`; the authors state plainly that "moving to sparse attention mechanism do incur a
    cost".
22. **No paper I retrieved equalises FLOPs between a truncation baseline and a long-context model**, and **no paper
    compares head+tail truncation against Longformer/BigBird/hierarchical on the same benchmark.** The head-to-head
    question is unresolved in the literature.
23. **LongBench has no document-classification category**, Loong and DocFinQA are QA, and arXiv/PubMed/BookSum are
    summarisation. Four of the named "long-document classification benchmarks" are not classification benchmarks.
24. **A widely-repeatable claim I had to retract**: "TF-IDF+SVM beats Longformer on LexGLUE/SCOTUS" is contradicted
    by LexGLUE's own v4 correction note (§5.2). Marked unresolved; do not cite.

## What R-003 most constrains (one-paragraph summary for the decision record)

The literature does not contain a clean answer to "does long-context machinery beat truncation for document
classification", because the two best head-to-head studies **disagree** and both carry confounds: Park et al. (ACL
2022) show truncation beating Longformer by 18.6 μ-F1 on EURLEX but used an anomalous 0.005 learning rate on
Longformer only, while Dai et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2022) show long-context models beating 512-token baselines by
13–15 F1 on MIMIC-III with a documented memory advantage for hierarchical designs. What *is* consistent is the
moderating variable: **the fraction of documents whose label-bearing evidence lies beyond the truncation budget**
(BigBird's "excess fraction": +0.2 F1 at 14% excess, +4.89 at 100% excess), and **whether truncation captures the
evidence at all** (Chalkidis 2019: BERT-512 beats a full-document HAN on EURLEX because the labels sit in the
header and recitals). For an 8 GB budget the published memory table is decisive and unflattering: at 4,096 tokens
and batch size 2, base-sized **Longformer needs 11.8–19.4 GB and does not fit, while a hierarchical chunk encoder
needs 7.8–12.2 GB and only the smallest configuration fits.** On the marker-token question, the honest finding is
that **the U-shaped "lost in the middle" evidence is decoder-only** — there is no published accuracy-vs-evidence-
position curve for any bidirectional encoder — but the encoder-side evidence that does exist points the same
direction: a RoPE-based prefix `[CLS]` head suffers long-term attention decay toward distant tokens, prefix-pooled
embeddings concentrate on early positions, and on long-context retrieval `[CLS]` loses ~19 points to mean pooling
while remaining the best choice at short lengths, with much of the loss recoverable by matching fine-tuning length
to evaluation length.
